\topic{force-one-form}{Force as a one-form, and what that makes impulse}

Reading anchor: Sutton and Biblarz, ninth edition, Section 2.1, printed
p.~26 / PDF p.~50 \cite{sutton}; Frankel, Overview Section O.f, ``Line
Integrals'', printed p.~xxxix \cite{frankel}; Hill and Peterson, Sections 1.2
and 7.2, printed pp.~4 and 277 \cite{hillpeterson}. The claim developed here is
that force is naturally a covector, that impulse inherits that status, and that
Sutton's Eqs.~2--1 through 2--6 are flat-space specialisations of the
force--displacement pairing. That pairing needs no metric; unit directions,
force magnitudes and conversion of a covector into a velocity vector do.

\subsection{Work is a pairing, not a dot product}
Let $M$ be a smooth manifold, the configuration space of the mechanical system.
At each $x\in M$ the tangent space $T_xM$ holds velocities and the cotangent
space $T^*_xM$ holds linear functionals on them. The evaluation
$T^*_xM\times T_xM\to\R$, $(\alpha,v)\mapsto\alpha(v)$, is canonical: it is
defined by the manifold structure alone and requires \emph{no} metric.

A position-dependent force is specified by its pairing with displacements.
For a curve
$C:[a,b]\to M$ with $t\mapsto x(t)$,
\[
 W=\int_C f=\int_a^b f\bigl(\dot x(t)\bigr)\,dt
  =\int_a^b f_k\bigl(x(t)\bigr)\frac{dx^k}{dt}\,dt ,
\]
which is the ordinary integral of the pullback $C^*f$ over $[a,b]$. The
integrand is a $1$-form $f=f_k\,dx^k\in\Omega^1(M)$, and the value is
independent of the parameterisation at fixed orientation. Frankel states the
conclusion directly: the integrand of a line integral is naturally a $1$-form,
not a vector, and the work integral ``does not require a metric''
\cite[Section O.f]{frankel}.

The position-only hypothesis matters here. A general applied force may depend
on velocity and time, $f(x,v,t)\in T_x^*M$. It is then a covector at $x$
parametrised by $v,t$, rather than a single one-form on $M$. Its work along
the actual motion is
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:VelocityDependentForceWork}
 W=\int_a^b f_k(x(t),\dot x(t),t)\dot x^k(t)\,dt.
\end{equation}
This quantity can change if the same geometric path is traversed at a
different speed. For example, on a line with $f=-b\dot x\,dx$, $b>0$, traverse
a distance $\ell>0$ uniformly in a time $\tau>0$. Then
\[
 \dot x=\frac{\ell}{\tau},\qquad
 W=-b\int_0^\tau\left(\frac{\ell}{\tau}\right)^2dt
   =-\frac{b\ell^2}{\tau}.
\]
Replacing $\tau$ by $2\tau$ halves the dissipated energy. This changes the
physical timing of the motion; it is distinct from changing the integration
variable while describing the same timed trajectory.

The familiar $W=\int\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r$ is therefore \emph{not} the
primitive statement. It presupposes a metric $g$ (to form the dot product) and
usually also Cartesian components (so that $g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}$ makes the
distinction invisible). Both are extra structure, and both are available in the
rocket problem --- which is precisely why the shortcut is safe there and unsafe
elsewhere.

\subsection{Why the index is down: the Legendre transform}
The covector status of force is not a convention; it follows from the
variational formulation. The fibre derivative and the variational principles
below require no metric. The main calculation assumes $L=T(x,\dot x)-V(x)$;
the later examples explain what changes when time or velocity dependence is
allowed in the potential. A particular metric kinetic energy is chosen in the
next subsection.

For the general form of the Lagrangian that only depends upon $x,\dot{x}$, and
assuming that $V=V(x)$ (i.e.\ potential $V$ has no dependency on $\dot{x}$),
then
\[
L = L(x,\dot{x}) = T(x,\dot{x}) - V(x)
\]
Also recall that momentum $p_k$ is defined in terms of the Lagrangian as
follows:
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:MomentumDefinitionFromLagrangian}
p_k := \frac{\partial L}{ \partial \dot{x}^k}
\end{equation}

So
\begin{align}
& \frac{\partial L }{ \partial x^i}
  = \frac{\partial T}{ \partial x^i} - \frac{ \partial V}{\partial x^i}
  \label{Eq:LagrangianFormPart2} \\
& \frac{\partial L }{ \partial \dot{x}^i}
  = \frac{\partial T}{ \partial \dot{x}^i}
  \label{Eq:LagrangianFormPart0} \\
& \Longrightarrow \frac{d}{dt} \frac{\partial L }{ \partial \dot{x}^i}
  = \frac{ \partial^2 T}{ \partial x^j \partial \dot{x}^i } \dot{x}^j
  + \frac{ \partial^2 T}{ \partial \dot{x}^j \partial \dot{x}^i } \ddot{x}^j
  = \dot{p}_i \label{Eq:LagrangianFormPart1}
\end{align}

\noindent where the last equality was obtained using
Eq.~\ref{Eq:MomentumDefinitionFromLagrangian}, the definition of momentum
$p_i$.

\subsubsection*{Where the two terms in the time derivative come from}
The quantity $\frac{\partial T}{\partial \dot x^i}$ is a function on $TM$: it has a slot
for the point and a slot for the velocity,
$(x,\dot x)\mapsto(\frac{\partial T}{\partial \dot x^i})(x,\dot x)$. Along a trajectory
both slots move with $t$, so differentiating in $t$ is the chain rule in $2n$
variables,
\[
 \frac{d}{dt}\Bigl[\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot x^i}
   \bigl(x(t),\dot x(t)\bigr)\Bigr]
 =\frac{\partial}{\partial x^j}
   \Bigl(\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot x^i}\Bigr)\frac{dx^j}{dt}
 +\frac{\partial}{\partial\dot x^j}
   \Bigl(\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot x^i}\Bigr)\frac{d\dot x^j}{dt},
\]
and Eq.~\ref{Eq:LagrangianFormPart1} is what this becomes once $dx^j/dt=\dot
x^j$ and $d\dot x^j/dt=\ddot x^j$ are substituted. The first term is present
whenever $\frac{\partial T}{\partial \dot x^i}$ depends on position. For the later
constant-mass example $T=\tfrac12m\delta_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j$ it vanishes
in Cartesian coordinates; a general $T(x,\dot x)$ need not have this property.
Nothing about the form of $T$ has been assumed here:
Eq.~\ref{Eq:LagrangianFormPart1} is the chain rule and nothing else.

If we consider the Euler--Lagrange equation, which could take the following
forms:
\[
\begin{aligned}
& \frac{d}{dt} \frac{\partial L }{ \partial \dot{x}^k}
  - \frac{\partial L }{ \partial x^k} = 0 \text{ or } \\
& \frac{d}{dt} \frac{\partial L }{ \partial \dot{x}^k}
  - \frac{\partial L }{ \partial x^k} = f_k
\end{aligned}
\]
where $f_k$ is some external force (or forces) applied on the total system that
has not been written into $L$ --- usually, though not necessarily,
non-conservative, which is settled two headings below --- then from the
Euler--Lagrange equation and Eqns.~\ref{Eq:LagrangianFormPart1},
\ref{Eq:LagrangianFormPart2}, we get
\[
\begin{aligned}
	& \dot{p}_k  -  \frac{\partial T}{ \partial x^k} - F_k = 0 \text{ or } \\
	& \dot{p}_k  -  \frac{\partial T}{ \partial x^k} - F_k = f_k  \\
\end{aligned}
\]
where $F_k := -\frac{\partial V}{\partial x^k}$ (i.e.\ the negative of the
gradient of a conservative potential is a conservative force due to the
potential). Written out, that substitution is
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}
   -\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}
 &=\dot p_k-\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}
 &&\text{by Eq.~\ref{Eq:LagrangianFormPart1}}\\
 &=\dot p_k-\frac{\partial T}{\partial x^k}
   +\frac{\partial V}{\partial x^k}
 &&\text{by Eq.~\ref{Eq:LagrangianFormPart2}}\\
 &=\dot p_k-\frac{\partial T}{\partial x^k}-F_k
 &&\text{by the definition of }F_k .
\end{aligned}
\]
We can combine the force due to the conservative potential and the
``external'' force that's not necessarily conservative into one force term:
\[
\mathfrak{F}_k \equiv f_k + F_k
\]
so that
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:EulerLagrangeEquationForm2}
	\dot{p}_k - \frac{\partial T}{ \partial x^k} = \mathfrak{F}_k
\end{equation}

Equation~\ref{Eq:EulerLagrangeEquationForm2} is the statement this section is
about. The momentum $p_k=\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot x^k}$ is the image of the
fibre derivative $\mathbb FL:TM\to T^*M$, and $F_k=-\frac{\partial V}{\partial x^k}$
are the components of $-dV$. Both are covectors at the configuration point.
However, $\dot p_k$ and $\frac{\partial T}{\partial x^k}$ separately are generally
\emph{not} covector components: $T$ depends on velocity as well as position.
The Euler--Lagrange combination has the covector transformation law.

To show the distinction, let $x=x(q)$ be a time-independent coordinate change,
and define
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:CoordinateJacobianForEL}
\begin{gathered}
 A^i{}_a:=\frac{\partial x^i}{\partial q^a},\qquad
 \dot x^i=A^i{}_a\dot q^a,\\
 \widetilde L(q,\dot q):=L\bigl(x(q),A(q)\dot q\bigr).
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
The chain rule, with $q$ fixed when differentiating in $\dot q$, gives
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:MomentumCovectorTransformation}
 \widetilde p_a:=\frac{\partial\widetilde L}{\partial\dot q^a}
 =\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^i}
   \frac{\partial\dot x^i}{\partial\dot q^a}
 =p_i A^i{}_a .
\end{equation}
For the position derivative, holding $\dot q$ fixed does not hold $\dot x$
fixed: Eq.~\ref{Eq:CoordinateJacobianForEL} gives
\[
 \frac{\partial\dot x^i}{\partial q^a}\Big|_{\dot q}
 =\frac{\partial}{\partial q^a}(A^i{}_b\dot q^b)
 =\frac{\partial A^i{}_b}{\partial q^a}\dot q^b.
\]
Differentiating Eq.~\ref{Eq:MomentumCovectorTransformation} in time, and
the definition of $\widetilde L$ in $q$ at fixed $\dot q$, respectively,
\begin{align}
 \frac{d\widetilde p_a}{dt}
 &=\frac{d}{dt}(p_iA^i{}_a)
 =A^i{}_a\dot p_i+p_i\frac{\partial A^i{}_a}{\partial q^b}\dot q^b,
 \label{Eq:MomentumDerivativeTransformation}\\
 \frac{\partial\widetilde L}{\partial q^a}
 &=\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^i}A^i{}_a
   +\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^i}
     \frac{\partial\dot x^i}{\partial q^a}\Big|_{\dot q}\notag\\
 &=A^i{}_a\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^i}
   +p_i\frac{\partial A^i{}_b}{\partial q^a}\dot q^b.
 \label{Eq:PositionDerivativeTransformation}
\end{align}
Mixed partial derivatives of the smooth coordinate map commute:
\[
 \frac{\partial A^i{}_a}{\partial q^b}
 =\frac{\partial^2x^i}{\partial q^b\partial q^a}
 =\frac{\partial^2x^i}{\partial q^a\partial q^b}
 =\frac{\partial A^i{}_b}{\partial q^a}.
\]
Subtracting Eq.~\ref{Eq:PositionDerivativeTransformation} from
Eq.~\ref{Eq:MomentumDerivativeTransformation} therefore cancels the extra terms:
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:ELCovectorTransformation}
\begin{aligned}
 \frac{d\widetilde p_a}{dt}-\frac{\partial\widetilde L}{\partial q^a}
 &=A^i{}_a\left(\dot p_i-\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^i}\right)\\
 &\quad+p_i\left(\frac{\partial A^i{}_a}{\partial q^b}
              -\frac{\partial A^i{}_b}{\partial q^a}\right)\dot q^b\\
 &=A^i{}_a\left(\dot p_i-\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^i}\right).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
This is the required covector law \cite[Section 2.2]{tongdynamics}.
For $L=T-V(x)$, Eq.~\ref{Eq:LagrangianFormPart0} gives
$p_i=\frac{\partial T}{\partial \dot x^i}$, so applying the same subtraction to $T$
gives, explicitly,
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:KineticELCovectorTransformation}
 \frac{d\widetilde p_a}{dt}-\frac{\partial\widetilde T}{\partial q^a}
 =A^i{}_a\left(\dot p_i-\frac{\partial T}{\partial x^i}\right).
\end{equation}
Finally, $-dV$ requires no metric;
the gradient of $V$ is $(dV)^\sharp$ and does require one.

\subsubsection*{Why the right-hand side is zero almost every time you see it}
The homogeneous form is what Hamilton's principle produces, and it is worth
seeing the boundary term in order to know what the zero is claiming. Fix
endpoints $x(t_0)=x_0$, $x(t_1)=x_1$, and let
\[
 S[x]=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}L\bigl(x(t),\dot x(t)\bigr)\,dt .
\]
Vary the curve by $x_\varepsilon^k(t)=x^k(t)+\varepsilon\eta^k(t)$ with
$\eta^k(t_0)=\eta^k(t_1)=0$, so the varied curve still joins the same two
endpoints. Then
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\Big|_{\varepsilon=0}S[x_\varepsilon]
 &=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\Bigl(\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}\eta^k
   +\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}\dot\eta^k\Bigr)dt\\
 &=\Bigl[\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}\eta^k\Bigr]_{t_0}^{t_1}
   -\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\Bigl(\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}
   -\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}\Bigr)\eta^k\,dt ,
\end{aligned}
\]
the second line by integrating the $\dot\eta^k$ term by parts, and the bracket
vanishes because $\eta$ was required to vanish at both endpoints. Stationarity
means what is left vanishes for \emph{every} admissible $\eta$, and the
fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations then forces the integrand's
bracket to vanish at every $t$:
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:EulerLagrangeHomogeneous}
 \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}
   -\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}=0 .
\end{equation}
Three consequences, together, explain the ubiquity of that zero.

\begin{description}\itemsep3pt
\item[The zero does not say ``no force''.] The bracket in
  Eq.~\ref{Eq:EulerLagrangeHomogeneous} is a covector, and its integral
  against an arbitrary variation $\eta$ is (minus) the first variation. Setting
  it to zero says there is no \emph{left-over} covector once everything has
  been accounted for inside $L$. A body falling under gravity has its force
  inside $V$, and its Euler--Lagrange equation still reads $=0$.
\item[Anything with a potential can be moved to the left.] The two ways of
  carrying a potential force give the same equation:
  \[
  \begin{aligned}
   \text{(i)}\ \ L_1&=T,\ \text{right side }F_k:
     &&\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot x^k}
       -\frac{\partial T}{\partial x^k}=-\frac{\partial V}{\partial x^k},\\
   \text{(ii)}\ \ L_2&=T-V,\ \text{right side }0:
     &&\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial(T-V)}{\partial\dot x^k}
       -\frac{\partial(T-V)}{\partial x^k}=0 .
  \end{aligned}
  \]
  Expanding (ii) with $\frac{\partial V}{\partial \dot x^k}=0$ gives
  $\dot p_k-\frac{\partial T}{\partial x^k}+\frac{\partial V}{\partial x^k}=0$, which is (i)
  rearranged. So the split $\mathfrak F_k=f_k+F_k$ is bookkeeping, chosen by
  the modeller; only the total $\mathfrak F_k$ is fixed by the problem. The
  standard convention is to push everything that admits a potential into $L$,
  which empties the right-hand side for exactly the systems textbooks like to
  choose.
\item[The homogeneous form gives the usual conservation laws.] Noether's
  theorem relates symmetries of the action to conserved quantities of its
  Euler--Lagrange flow. For a regular $L$ (nonsingular velocity Hessian), the
  Legendre map also gives a local Hamiltonian formulation. Adding a force
  does not remove that map or the canonical symplectic form on $T^*M$;
  whether the forced flow preserves the form or a particular momentum must
  be checked from the force, as the balance below illustrates.
\end{description}

For forces kept outside the chosen $L$, stationarity of $S[x]$ alone does
not give the forced equation. The Lagrange--d'Alembert principle instead asks
that the variation of this action plus the virtual work of those forces vanish:
\[
 \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\Bigl(\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}\eta^k
  +\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}\dot\eta^k
  +f_k\eta^k\Bigr)dt=0
 \quad\text{for all }\eta\text{ with }\eta(t_0)=\eta(t_1)=0 .
\]
Integrating by parts and using the endpoint conditions gives explicitly
\[
\begin{aligned}
 0&=[p_k\eta^k]_{t_0}^{t_1}
   +\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}
        -\dot p_k+f_k\right)\eta^k\,dt\\
  &=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}
 \left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}-\frac{d}{dt}
 \frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}+f_k\right)\eta^k\,dt .
\end{aligned}
\]
Since the variations are arbitrary, the fundamental lemma yields
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:EulerLagrangeForced}
 \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}
 -\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}=f_k .
\end{equation}
The term $f_k\eta^k$ is virtual work: a
covector paired with a displacement, the same pairing as in the first
subsection, with no metric used anywhere. That is the second and independent
reason the index on $f$ is down --- $f$ is \emph{defined} by what it does to a
virtual displacement.

It is also what fixes how a generalised force transforms. If the system is a
collection of particles carrying ambient force components $F^{(a)}_i$ at
positions $r_{(a)}(q)$, and $q^k$ are any configuration coordinates, then
\[
 \delta W=\sum_a F^{(a)}_i\,\delta r^i_{(a)}
  =\sum_a F^{(a)}_i\frac{\partial r^i_{(a)}}{\partial q^k}\,\delta q^k
  =:Q_k\,\delta q^k ,
 \qquad
 Q_k=\sum_a F^{(a)}_i\frac{\partial r^i_{(a)}}{\partial q^k},
\]
and $\frac{\partial r^i}{\partial q^k}$ is precisely the Jacobian appearing in the
transformation law of a covector. A torque showing up as the $\theta$ slot of a
force covector, carrying an extra factor of $r$, is this formula and nothing
more.

The force also determines an energy balance. Define $E:=p_k\dot x^k-L$.
If $T$ is homogeneous of degree two in velocity, then for a scale factor
$\lambda$,
\[
\begin{aligned}
 T(x,\lambda\dot x)&=\lambda^2T(x,\dot x),\\
 \frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot x^k}\dot x^k
 &=\left.\frac{d}{d\lambda}T(x,\lambda\dot x)\right|_{\lambda=1}
 =\left.2\lambda T(x,\dot x)\right|_{\lambda=1}=2T.
\end{aligned}
\]
For $V=V(x)$, Eq.~\ref{Eq:LagrangianFormPart0} therefore gives
$E=2T-(T-V)=T+V$. Along any solution,
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \frac{dE}{dt}
 &=\Bigl(\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}\Bigr)\dot x^k
   +\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}\ddot x^k
   -\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}\dot x^k
   -\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}\ddot x^k\\
 &=\Bigl(\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}
   -\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}\Bigr)\dot x^k
  =f_k\dot x^k=f(\dot x) .
\end{aligned}
\]
For the time-independent $L$ used here, the homogeneous form conserves $E$.
In the forced form, $f_k\dot x^k$ is the power supplied by the additional force;
it may be positive, negative, or zero. In particular, $f\ne0$ does not imply
$dE/dt\ne0$.

A symmetry can also retain its conserved momentum in a forced system. For
example, consider a time-independent infinitesimal transformation
$x_\varepsilon^k=x^k+\varepsilon\xi^k(x)+O(\varepsilon^2)$. Its induced
velocity variation is
\[
 \left.\frac{\partial\dot x_\varepsilon^k}{\partial\varepsilon}
 \right|_{\varepsilon=0}
 =\frac{d}{dt}\xi^k(x(t))
 =\frac{\partial\xi^k}{\partial x^j}\dot x^j.
\]
Strict invariance means that the first-order change of $L$ vanishes:
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:StrictSymmetryCondition}
 0=\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\varepsilon}
 L(x_\varepsilon,\dot x_\varepsilon)\right|_{\varepsilon=0}
 =\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}\xi^k
   +p_k\frac{\partial\xi^k}{\partial x^j}\dot x^j .
\end{equation}
Using Eq.~\ref{Eq:EulerLagrangeForced}, then
Eq.~\ref{Eq:StrictSymmetryCondition}, gives the momentum balance
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:ForcedSymmetryBalance}
\begin{aligned}
 \frac{d}{dt}(p_k\xi^k)
 &=\dot p_k\xi^k+p_k\frac{\partial\xi^k}{\partial x^j}\dot x^j\\
 &=\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}+f_k\right)\xi^k
   +p_k\frac{\partial\xi^k}{\partial x^j}\dot x^j\\
 &=\underbrace{\left(\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}\xi^k
     +p_k\frac{\partial\xi^k}{\partial x^j}\dot x^j\right)}_{0\text{ by Eq.~\ref{Eq:StrictSymmetryCondition}}}
     +f_k\xi^k
 =f_k\xi^k .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Thus $p_k\xi^k$ is conserved when the force has zero pairing with this symmetry
along the solution. This is the strict configuration-symmetry case of the
forced Noether balance \cite{deleonforced}; no assertion that every forced
system has a nontrivial conserved momentum is needed.

\subsubsection*{What forcing changes in the symplectic calculation}
The distinction between a symplectic form and its preservation can also be
checked directly. For a regular $L$, solve $p_i=\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot x^i}$
locally for $\dot x=v(x,p,t)$ and define
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:LocalHamiltonianDefinition}
 H(x,p,t):=p_iv^i-L(x,v,t).
\end{equation}
Here regularity means the velocity Hessian
$\frac{\partial^2L}{\partial\dot x^i\,\partial\dot x^j}$ is nonsingular; it is the
hypothesis that allows this local inverse. In the following exterior
derivatives, $t$ is held fixed. Differentiating
Eq.~\ref{Eq:LocalHamiltonianDefinition} gives
\[
\begin{aligned}
 dH&=v^i\,dp_i+p_i\,dv^i
     -\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^i}\,dx^i
     -\frac{\partial L}{\partial v^i}\,dv^i\\
   &=v^i\,dp_i-\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^i}\,dx^i,
   \qquad p_i=\frac{\partial L}{\partial v^i}.
\end{aligned}
\]
Matching coefficients and using Eq.~\ref{Eq:EulerLagrangeForced},
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:ForcedHamiltonEquations}
 \dot x^i=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i},\qquad
 \dot p_i=\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^i}+f_i
         =-\frac{\partial H}{\partial x^i}+f_i.
\end{equation}
The force is now evaluated at $(x,v(x,p,t),t)$. On $T^*M$, define the
canonical one-form $\vartheta=p_i\,dx^i$ and its symplectic form
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:CanonicalSymplecticForm}
 \omega=-d\vartheta=-dp_i\wedge dx^i=dx^i\wedge dp_i,
 \qquad d\omega=-d^2\vartheta=0.
\end{equation}
These definitions do not involve $f$. The time-dependent vector field of
Eq.~\ref{Eq:ForcedHamiltonEquations} is
\[
 X=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}
   +\left(-\frac{\partial H}{\partial x^i}+f_i\right)
     \frac{\partial}{\partial p_i}.
\]
The contraction $\iota_X\omega$ means inserting $X$ into the first slot of
$\omega$. Using
$\iota_X(dx^i\wedge dp_i)=X(x^i)\,dp_i-X(p_i)\,dx^i$, we obtain
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \iota_X\omega
 &=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}\,dp_i
   -\left(-\frac{\partial H}{\partial x^i}+f_i\right)dx^i\\
 &=dH-f_i\,dx^i.
\end{aligned}
\]
Cartan's formula gives the change of a differential form along the flow:
$\mathcal L_X\omega=d(\iota_X\omega)+\iota_X(d\omega)$. Thus
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:ForcedSymplecticBalance}
\begin{aligned}
 \mathcal L_X\omega
 &=d(dH-f_i\,dx^i)+\iota_X(0)\\
 &=-d(f_i\,dx^i)\\
 &=-\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x^j}\,dx^j\wedge dx^i
   -\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial p_j}\,dp_j\wedge dx^i.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
If $\Phi_{t,t_0}$ is the flow map carrying initial phase points at $t_0$ to
their values at $t$, differentiation of the pulled-back form gives
\[
 \frac{d}{dt}\Phi_{t,t_0}^*\omega
   =\Phi_{t,t_0}^*(\mathcal L_{X(t)}\omega).
\]
Thus $\mathcal L_{X(t)}\omega=0$ at every time implies
$\Phi_{t,t_0}^*\omega=\Phi_{t_0,t_0}^*\omega=\omega$.
A general force need not satisfy this preservation condition. The form
itself still exists in either case.

For an explicit example, keep a spring force on the right in one dimension:
$L=\tfrac12m\dot x^2$, $p=m\dot x$, $f=-\kappa x$, with $m,\kappa>0$.
Then $H=p^2/(2m)$ and Eq.~\ref{Eq:ForcedHamiltonEquations} reads
\[
 \dot x=\frac{p}{m},\qquad \dot p=-\kappa x.
\]
Equation~\ref{Eq:ForcedSymplecticBalance} gives
$\mathcal L_X\omega=-d(-\kappa x\,dx)=\kappa\,dx\wedge dx=0$.
Moreover, define $H_{\rm total}=p^2/(2m)+\kappa x^2/2$. Its derivative is
\[
 \frac{dH_{\rm total}}{dt}
 =\frac{p}{m}\dot p+\kappa x\dot x
 =\frac{p}{m}(-\kappa x)+\kappa x\frac{p}{m}=0.
\]
Thus a nonzero force on the right is compatible with both a conserved total
energy and a symplectic flow. The physical equations decide these properties.

\subsubsection*{Which forces may be moved into the Lagrangian}
The parenthetical above deserves a definite answer, because three different
notions get collapsed into the one word ``conservative''.

\begin{description}\itemsep3pt
\item[Conservative means exact.] A force one-form $f=f_k\,dx^k$ on
  configuration space is conservative when it is exact, $f=-dV$ for some
  $V=V(x)$. Equivalently, the work $\int_Cf$ depends only on the endpoints of
  $C$; equivalently again, $f$ is closed, $\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x^j}=\frac{\partial f_j}{\partial x^i}$, \emph{provided} the domain is simply connected
  (Poincar\'e's lemma). The proviso is not idle: on the punctured plane the
  one-form written $d\theta$ --- by habit, since $\theta$ is not a global
  function there --- is closed but not exact, so a force proportional to it
  passes the ``curl-free'' test pointwise and still does net work around a
  loop.
\item[Time dependence requires a separate energy check.] If $V=V(x,t)$,
  then $F=-d_xV$ is spatially exact at each fixed time. Allowing $L=L(x,\dot x,t)$
  adds one term to the chain rule in the energy calculation:
  \begin{equation}\label{Eq:ForcedTimeDependentEnergyBalance}
  \begin{aligned}
   \frac{dE}{dt}
   &=\dot p_k\dot x^k+p_k\ddot x^k
     -\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}\dot x^k
     -\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}\ddot x^k
     -\frac{\partial L}{\partial t}\\
   &=\left(\dot p_k-\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}\right)\dot x^k
     +\left(p_k-\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}\right)\ddot x^k
     -\frac{\partial L}{\partial t}\\
   &=f_k\dot x^k-\frac{\partial L}{\partial t}.
  \end{aligned}
  \end{equation}
  For time-independent $T$ and $f=0$, this gives $d(T+V)/dt=\frac{\partial V}{\partial t}$,
  so $T+V$ is not generally conserved. Spatial exactness at fixed time and
  conservation of mechanical energy are distinct statements. Here the word
  conservative uses the time-independent convention of the preceding item;
  a time-dependent potential remains admissible in $L$.
  Even explicit time dependence of $V$ need not make the force time-dependent:
  if $V(x,t)=V_0(x)+a(t)$, then
  \[
  \begin{aligned}
   F_k&=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x^k}(V_0+a)
       =-\frac{\partial V_0}{\partial x^k},\\
   E&=T+V_0+a(t),\qquad \frac{dE}{dt}=a'(t),\\
   \frac{d}{dt}(T+V_0)&=\frac{dE}{dt}-a'(t)=0
       \qquad(f=0).
  \end{aligned}
  \]
  Here $a(t)$ changes the reference of the energy function without changing
  the force or the conservation of $T+V_0$.
\item[Velocity dependence need not break it.] The hypothesis $V=V(x)$ is
  exactly what makes $\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot x^i}=\frac{\partial T}{\partial \dot x^i}$
  in Eq.~\ref{Eq:LagrangianFormPart0}. It can be weakened: a force is derivable
  from a \emph{generalised} potential $U(x,\dot x,t)$ when
  \begin{equation}\label{Eq:GeneralisedPotentialForce}
   \mathfrak F_k=\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial U}{\partial\dot x^k}
     -\frac{\partial U}{\partial x^k},
  \end{equation}
  and then $L=T-U$ restores the homogeneous form. For the Lorentz force,
  take $U=q\varphi-qA_i\dot x^i$, where $q$ is the particle's charge.
  Holding $x,t$ fixed in the velocity
  derivative gives
  \begin{equation}\label{Eq:ElectromagneticCanonicalMomentum}
  \begin{aligned}
   \frac{\partial U}{\partial\dot x^k}
      &=\frac{\partial}{\partial\dot x^k}(q\varphi-qA_i\dot x^i)
       =-qA_i\delta^i_k=-qA_k,\\
   p_k=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}
      &=\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot x^k}+qA_k
       =m\,g_{kj}\dot x^j+qA_k.
  \end{aligned}
  \end{equation}
  The last equality uses the metric kinetic energy computed in the next
  subsection. It exhibits the shift from kinetic to canonical momentum.
  More generally, define the kinetic momentum $\pi_k:=\frac{\partial T}{\partial \dot x^k}$.
  Then for $L=T-U$,
  \begin{equation}\label{Eq:CanonicalAndKineticMomentum}
   p_k=\frac{\partial(T-U)}{\partial\dot x^k}
      =\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot x^k}
        -\frac{\partial U}{\partial\dot x^k}
      =\pi_k-\frac{\partial U}{\partial\dot x^k}.
  \end{equation}
  The momenta coincide wherever $\frac{\partial U}{\partial \dot x^k}=0$ for every $k$;
  time dependence alone, as in $U=V(x,t)$, does not separate them. When the
  velocity derivative is nonzero, Eq.~\ref{Eq:LagrangianFormPart0} and the
  equations derived using it must be revised. If $U$ represents all the force,
  the homogeneous equation explicitly becomes
  \[
  \begin{aligned}
   0&=\frac{d}{dt}\left(\pi_k-\frac{\partial U}{\partial\dot x^k}\right)
     -\left(\frac{\partial T}{\partial x^k}-\frac{\partial U}{\partial x^k}\right)\\
    &=\dot\pi_k-\frac{\partial T}{\partial x^k}
       -\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial U}{\partial\dot x^k}
       +\frac{\partial U}{\partial x^k}.
  \end{aligned}
  \]
  Moving the last two terms to the other side and using
  Eq.~\ref{Eq:GeneralisedPotentialForce},
  \begin{equation}\label{Eq:GeneralisedPotentialKineticBalance}
   \dot\pi_k-\frac{\partial T}{\partial x^k}
    =\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial U}{\partial\dot x^k}
      -\frac{\partial U}{\partial x^k}=\mathfrak F_k.
  \end{equation}
  Covector character survives, with the kinetic force balance written using
  $\pi_k$.

  The Lorentz-force substitution in Eq.~\ref{Eq:GeneralisedPotentialForce}
  is also explicit. With $A_i=A_i(x,t)$ and $\varphi=\varphi(x,t)$,
  \begin{equation}\label{Eq:LorentzForceFromGeneralisedPotential}
  \begin{aligned}
   \mathfrak F_k
    &=\frac{d}{dt}(-qA_k)
      -\frac{\partial}{\partial x^k}(q\varphi-qA_j\dot x^j)\\
    &=-q\left(\frac{\partial A_k}{\partial t}
          +\frac{\partial A_k}{\partial x^j}\dot x^j\right)
      -q\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x^k}
      +q\frac{\partial A_j}{\partial x^k}\dot x^j\\
    &=q\left(-\frac{\partial A_k}{\partial t}
             -\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x^k}\right)
      +q\left(\frac{\partial A_j}{\partial x^k}
             -\frac{\partial A_k}{\partial x^j}\right)\dot x^j.
  \end{aligned}
  \end{equation}
  Define $B_{kj}:=\frac{\partial A_j}{\partial x^k}-\frac{\partial A_k}{\partial x^j}$;
  then $B_{jk}=-B_{kj}$. The magnetic power vanishes because
  \[
  \begin{aligned}
   P_{\rm mag}&=qB_{kj}\dot x^j\dot x^k
     =qB_{jk}\dot x^k\dot x^j
     =-qB_{kj}\dot x^j\dot x^k=-P_{\rm mag},\\
   2P_{\rm mag}&=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad P_{\rm mag}=0.
  \end{aligned}
  \]
  The second equality renames the summed indices; the third uses the
  antisymmetry of $B$ and commutation of the velocity components. A nonzero
  magnetic force depends on velocity and is not a position-only exact
  one-form $-dV(x)$, despite doing no work.
\item[Rayleigh dissipation keeps the mechanical Lagrangian fixed.] For a linear
  damper $f_k=-C_{kj}\dot x^j$, with $C=C(x,t)$ symmetric and positive
  semi-definite, the standard formulation keeps $L=T-V$ and uses the
  Rayleigh dissipation function
  $\mathcal R=\tfrac12C_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j$. At fixed $x,t$,
  \begin{equation}\label{Eq:RayleighForceAndPower}
  \begin{aligned}
   \frac{\partial\mathcal R}{\partial\dot x^k}
    &=\frac12C_{ij}(\delta^i_k\dot x^j+\dot x^i\delta^j_k)\\
    &=\frac12(C_{kj}\dot x^j+C_{ik}\dot x^i)
     =C_{kj}\dot x^j,\\
   f_k&=-\frac{\partial\mathcal R}{\partial\dot x^k}
       =-C_{kj}\dot x^j,\\
   f_k\dot x^k&=-C_{kj}\dot x^k\dot x^j=-2\mathcal R\le0.
  \end{aligned}
  \end{equation}
  The second line renames $i$ to $j$ and uses $C_{jk}=C_{kj}$; the inequality
  uses positive semi-definiteness. The Rayleigh function supplies a force
  on the right and is not added to the mechanical $L$.

  The restriction on a generalised potential has a precise scope. If a smooth
  $U(x,\dot x,t)$ must reproduce the force as an identity without acceleration
  terms while $T$ is fixed, expand Eq.~\ref{Eq:GeneralisedPotentialForce}:
  \begin{equation}\label{Eq:GeneralisedPotentialAccelerationTerms}
  \begin{aligned}
   f_k&=\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial U}{\partial\dot x^k}
       -\frac{\partial U}{\partial x^k}\\
      &=\frac{\partial^2U}{\partial\dot x^k\partial\dot x^j}\ddot x^j
    +\frac{\partial^2U}{\partial\dot x^k\partial x^j}\dot x^j
    +\frac{\partial^2U}{\partial\dot x^k\partial t}
    -\frac{\partial U}{\partial x^k}.
  \end{aligned}
  \end{equation}
  Since this must be an identity for all accelerations at fixed $x,\dot x,t$,
  each acceleration coefficient must vanish:
  \[
   \frac{\partial^2U}{\partial\dot x^k\partial\dot x^j}=0
   \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
   \frac{\partial U}{\partial\dot x^k}=a_k(x,t)
   \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
   U=a_j(x,t)\dot x^j+b(x,t)
  \]
  locally on a connected velocity neighbourhood. In the partial derivatives
  below, $x$ and $\dot x$ are independent coordinates: a time partial
  holds both fixed, and a position partial holds the other coordinates
  fixed. The subscripts on $a_k$ and $f_k$ label covector components.
  Substitution into
  Eq.~\ref{Eq:GeneralisedPotentialForce} gives
  \begin{equation}\label{Eq:AffinePotentialForce}
  \begin{aligned}
   f_k&=\frac{d a_k}{dt}
        -\frac{\partial}{\partial x^k}(a_j\dot x^j+b)\\
      &=\frac{\partial a_k}{\partial t}+\frac{\partial a_k}{\partial x^j}\dot x^j
        -\frac{\partial a_j}{\partial x^k}\dot x^j-\frac{\partial b}{\partial x^k}\\
      &=\left(\frac{\partial a_k}{\partial x^j}
             -\frac{\partial a_j}{\partial x^k}\right)\dot x^j
        +\frac{\partial a_k}{\partial t}-\frac{\partial b}{\partial x^k}.
  \end{aligned}
  \end{equation}
  Let $D_{kj}:=\frac{\partial a_k}{\partial x^j}-\frac{\partial a_j}{\partial x^k}$. Its transpose is
  $D_{jk}=\frac{\partial a_j}{\partial x^k}-\frac{\partial a_k}{\partial x^j}=-D_{kj}$. Equality to the damping
  law for all velocities would require $D_{kj}=-C_{kj}$ and a zero
  velocity-independent term. But symmetry of $C$ then gives
  \[
   D_{kj}=-C_{kj}=-C_{jk}=D_{jk}=-D_{kj}
   \quad\Longrightarrow\quad D_{kj}=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad C_{kj}=0.
  \]
  Thus a nonzero symmetric linear damping law is excluded under the specified
  additive-potential assumptions.

  This does not rule out a different Lagrangian for the same trajectories.
  For a constant mass $m>0$, damping rate $\gamma>0$, and a one-dimensional
  potential $V(x)$, the Bateman--Caldirola--Kanai construction is
  \cite{leonegourieux}
  \begin{equation}\label{Eq:DampedParticleAlternativeLagrangian}
   L_d=e^{\gamma t}\left(\tfrac12m\dot x^2-V(x)\right).
  \end{equation}
  Each derivative in its Euler--Lagrange equation is
  \begin{equation}\label{Eq:DampedParticleLagrangianDerivatives}
  \begin{aligned}
   p_d=\frac{\partial L_d}{\partial\dot x}&=e^{\gamma t}m\dot x,\\
   \frac{d p_d}{dt}
      &=\gamma e^{\gamma t}m\dot x+e^{\gamma t}m\ddot x,\\
   \frac{\partial L_d}{\partial x}&=-e^{\gamma t}V'(x).
  \end{aligned}
  \end{equation}
  Subtracting the last line from the second,
  \[
  \begin{aligned}
   \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L_d}{\partial\dot x}
    -\frac{\partial L_d}{\partial x}
   &=e^{\gamma t}m\ddot x+\gamma e^{\gamma t}m\dot x+e^{\gamma t}V'(x)\\
   &=e^{\gamma t}\left(m\ddot x+m\gamma\dot x+V'(x)\right)=0 .
  \end{aligned}
  \]
  Since $e^{\gamma t}>0$, division gives
  $m\ddot x=-V'(x)-m\gamma\dot x$. Its mechanical energy obeys
  \begin{equation}\label{Eq:DampedParticleMechanicalEnergy}
   \frac{d}{dt}\left(\tfrac12m\dot x^2+V(x)\right)
   =(m\ddot x+V'(x))\dot x=-m\gamma\dot x^2\le0.
  \end{equation}
  The alternative Lagrangian therefore reproduces the dissipative trajectory
  without restoring conservation of mechanical energy. Its canonical momentum
  $p_d=e^{\gamma t}m\dot x$ is different from the mechanical momentum
  $p_{\rm mech}=m\dot x$. Its Hamiltonian follows by solving for the velocity:
  \begin{equation}\label{Eq:DampedParticleHamiltonian}
  \begin{aligned}
   \dot x&=e^{-\gamma t}\frac{p_d}{m},\\
   H_d=p_d\dot x-L_d
      &=e^{-\gamma t}\frac{p_d^2}{m}
        -\left(e^{-\gamma t}\frac{p_d^2}{2m}-e^{\gamma t}V(x)\right)\\
      &=e^{-\gamma t}\frac{p_d^2}{2m}+e^{\gamma t}V(x).
  \end{aligned}
  \end{equation}
  This time-dependent Hamiltonian has canonical form $dx\wedge dp_d$.
  For comparison, in the mechanical variables the force outside $T-V$ is
  $f=-\gamma p_{\rm mech}$, so Eq.~\ref{Eq:ForcedSymplecticBalance} gives
  \[
  \begin{aligned}
   \mathcal L_X(dx\wedge dp_{\rm mech})
    &=-d(-\gamma p_{\rm mech}\,dx)
     =\gamma\,dp_{\rm mech}\wedge dx\\
    &=-\gamma\,dx\wedge dp_{\rm mech}.
  \end{aligned}
  \]
  Thus the physical flow does not preserve this mechanical symplectic form.
  There is no contradiction with the alternative canonical formulation: at
  fixed time $dp_d=e^{\gamma t}dp_{\rm mech}$, hence
  $dx\wedge dp_d=e^{\gamma t}dx\wedge dp_{\rm mech}$; the change of momentum
  itself depends explicitly on time.
\item[Thrust is kept on the right in the vehicle model.] Rocket thrust is
  normally specified by propellant flow, exhaust conditions and control inputs,
  so the model here treats it as an applied force. This is a modelling choice:
  a prescribed spatially uniform force $f_k(t)$ can formally be represented by
  $U(x,t)=-f_k(t)x^k$ in Cartesian coordinates, since
  \[
   -\frac{\partial U}{\partial x^j}
    =-\frac{\partial}{\partial x^j}(-f_k(t)x^k)
    =f_k(t)\delta^k_j=f_j(t).
  \]
  That representation does not
  supply a rocket's open-system momentum balance. The changing vehicle mass
  and exhaust momentum flux still require the separate treatment below.
\end{description}

\noindent So the honest reading of $f_k$ is: whatever has not been put into
$L$. The split is a modelling choice; the total $\mathfrak F_k$, and its
covector character, are not.

\subsection{A point particle: kinetic energy built from a metric}
We now return to $L=T(x,\dot x)-V(x)$. Choosing the kinetic energy of a point
particle brings in the metric and gives the extra term
$\frac{\partial T}{\partial x^k}$ of Eq.~\ref{Eq:EulerLagrangeEquationForm2} a name.
Two hypotheses are used below and both matter: the metric $g$ is Riemannian
(symmetric, positive definite) with components $g_{ij}=g_{ij}(x)$ depending on
position only, and the mass $m$ is constant, since otherwise $m$ cannot be
pulled through $d/dt$.

Indeed, let's show how these equations are fulfilled for the kinetic energy
term $T$ having the following form:
\[
T = \frac{1}{2} m g_{ij} \dot{x}^i \dot{x}^j
\]
For a metric $g_{ij} = g_{ij}(x)$ dependent upon coordinates $x$, and symmetric
in $i,j$, then
\begin{align}
& \frac{ \partial T}{\partial x^k}
  = \frac{m}{2} \left( \frac{ \partial g_{ij}}{ \partial x^k}
    \dot{x}^i \dot{x}^j \right)
  \label{Eq:PartialDerivativeOfPointParticleKineticEnergy} \\
& \frac{ \partial T}{ \partial \dot{x}^k} = m g_{kj} \dot{x}^j
  \label{Eq:MomentumOfPointParticle}
\end{align}

\subsubsection*{Both of those derivatives, in full}
In Eq.~\ref{Eq:PartialDerivativeOfPointParticleKineticEnergy} only $g_{ij}$
carries an $x$: on $TM$ the velocities $\dot x^i$ are coordinates on the fibre,
independent of the $x^k$ and held fixed by $\frac{\partial}{\partial x^k}$. The other
one needs the product rule and $\frac{\partial\dot x^i}{\partial\dot x^k}=\delta^i_k$:
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot x^k}
 &=\frac{m}{2}\,g_{ij}\frac{\partial}{\partial\dot x^k}
    \bigl(\dot x^i\dot x^j\bigr)
  =\frac{m}{2}\,g_{ij}\bigl(\delta^i_k\dot x^j+\dot x^i\delta^j_k\bigr)\\
 &=\frac{m}{2}\bigl(g_{kj}\dot x^j+g_{ik}\dot x^i\bigr)
  =\frac{m}{2}\bigl(g_{kj}\dot x^j+g_{kj}\dot x^j\bigr)
  =m\,g_{kj}\dot x^j ,
\end{aligned}
\]
the last step renaming the dummy $i\to j$ and then using $g_{jk}=g_{kj}$.
Here metric symmetry makes the two product-rule terms equal.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:momentum-covector}
For $T=\tfrac12m\,g_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j$ and $V=V(x)$, the momentum conjugate
to $x^k$ is
$p_k=\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot x^k}=\frac{\partial T}{\partial \dot x^k}=m\,g_{kj}\dot x^j$;
that is, $p=m\,v^\flat$, the metric dual of $m$ times the velocity.
\end{proposition}

\noindent The proposition is Eq.~\ref{Eq:MomentumOfPointParticle} combined with
Eq.~\ref{Eq:MomentumDefinitionFromLagrangian} and
Eq.~\ref{Eq:LagrangianFormPart0}. Frankel performs exactly this calculation and
concludes that $p$ ``is $m$ times the covariant version of the velocity
vector'' \cite[Section O.f]{frankel}. The fibre derivative produces a covector
for any $L$. For this particular $L$, the metric is already part of $T$ and
determines the map $v\mapsto m\,g(v,\cdot)=m\,v^\flat$.

Differentiating Eq.~\ref{Eq:MomentumOfPointParticle} once more --- with respect
to $x^k$ only the metric responds, with respect to $\dot x^k$ only the explicit
$\dot x^j$ does --- gives
\[
\Longrightarrow \quad
\begin{aligned}
& \frac{ \partial^2 T}{ \partial \dot{x}^i \partial x^k}
  = m \left( \frac{\partial g_{ij} }{ \partial x^k}  \dot{x}^j \right)\\
& \frac{ \partial^2 T}{ \partial \dot{x}^j \partial \dot{x}^k } = m g_{jk}
\end{aligned}
\]
then from Eq.~\ref{Eq:LagrangianFormPart1}, we get
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:MomentumInTermsOfMetricAndCoordinates}
\dot{p}_k = m \left( \frac{\partial g_{ik} }{ \partial x^j}
  \dot{x}^j \dot{x}^i + g_{kj} \ddot{x}^j \right)
\end{equation}
Explicitly, that substitution is
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \dot p_k
 &=\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial x^j\partial\dot x^k}\dot x^j
  +\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial\dot x^j\partial\dot x^k}\ddot x^j
 &&\text{Eq.~\ref{Eq:LagrangianFormPart1} with }i\to k\\
 &=m\frac{\partial g_{ki}}{\partial x^j}\dot x^i\dot x^j
  +m\,g_{kj}\ddot x^j
 &&\text{the two second derivatives}\\
 &=m\Bigl(\frac{\partial g_{ik}}{\partial x^j}\dot x^j\dot x^i
  +g_{kj}\ddot x^j\Bigr)
 &&g_{ki}=g_{ik} .
\end{aligned}
\]
The direct product-rule route provides a check on the same answer:
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(m\,g_{kj}(x(t))\dot x^j(t)\bigr)
  &=m\frac{dg_{kj}(x(t))}{dt}\dot x^j+m\,g_{kj}\ddot x^j\\
  &=m\frac{\partial g_{kj}}{\partial x^i}\dot x^i\dot x^j
     +m\,g_{kj}\ddot x^j\\
  &=m\frac{\partial g_{ki}}{\partial x^j}\dot x^j\dot x^i
     +m\,g_{kj}\ddot x^j.
\end{aligned}
\]
The last line exchanges the dummy indices $i,j$ in the first term only.
Using $g_{ki}=g_{ik}$ gives
Eq.~\ref{Eq:MomentumInTermsOfMetricAndCoordinates} term for term.

Because $g_{kj}$ is symmetric in $k,j$ then we can write
\[
\frac{ \partial g_{kj}}{\partial x^i} \dot{x}^i \dot{x}^j
 = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{ \partial g_{kj}}{ \partial x^i}
   + \frac{ \partial g_{ki}}{\partial x^j} \right) \dot{x}^i \dot{x}^j
\]
The identity is right, but the symmetry doing the work is that of $\dot x^i\dot
x^j$ under $i\leftrightarrow j$, not that of $g$. Written out: $i$ and $j$ are
summed dummies, so renaming them changes nothing,
\[
 \frac{\partial g_{kj}}{\partial x^i}\dot x^i\dot x^j
 =\frac{\partial g_{ki}}{\partial x^j}\dot x^j\dot x^i
 =\frac{\partial g_{ki}}{\partial x^j}\dot x^i\dot x^j ,
\]
the last step only because the numbers $\dot x^i$ and $\dot x^j$ commute.
Averaging the first and last expressions gives the displayed identity.
This symmetrisation of the contracted indices does not require metric
symmetry, although metric symmetry is used elsewhere in the derivation.

\noindent And recall the definition of this form of the Christoffel symbol:
\[
	\Gamma_{k, ij} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{ \partial g_{kj}}{ \partial x^i}
   + \frac{\partial g_{ki} }{ \partial x^j}
   - \frac{ \partial g_{ij} }{ \partial x^k} \right)
\]
then combining the terms in
Eq.~\ref{Eq:PartialDerivativeOfPointParticleKineticEnergy},
Eq.~\ref{Eq:MomentumInTermsOfMetricAndCoordinates}, definition of
$\Gamma_{k,ij}$ into Eq.~\ref{Eq:EulerLagrangeEquationForm2}, we get
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \mathfrak F_k
 &=\dot p_k-\frac{\partial T}{\partial x^k}\\
 &=m\Bigl(\frac{\partial g_{ki}}{\partial x^j}\dot x^i\dot x^j
   +g_{kj}\ddot x^j\Bigr)
   -\frac{m}{2}\frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial x^k}\dot x^i\dot x^j\\
 &=m\,g_{kj}\ddot x^j
   +\frac{m}{2}\Bigl(\frac{\partial g_{kj}}{\partial x^i}
     +\frac{\partial g_{ki}}{\partial x^j}\Bigr)\dot x^i\dot x^j
   -\frac{m}{2}\frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial x^k}\dot x^i\dot x^j\\
 &=m\,g_{kj}\ddot x^j
   +\frac{m}{2}\Bigl(\frac{\partial g_{kj}}{\partial x^i}
     +\frac{\partial g_{ki}}{\partial x^j}
     -\frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial x^k}\Bigr)\dot x^i\dot x^j\\
 &=m\bigl(g_{kj}\ddot x^j+\Gamma_{k,ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j\bigr),
\end{aligned}
\]
line by line: the first is Eq.~\ref{Eq:EulerLagrangeEquationForm2}; the second
substitutes Eq.~\ref{Eq:MomentumInTermsOfMetricAndCoordinates} and
Eq.~\ref{Eq:PartialDerivativeOfPointParticleKineticEnergy}; the third replaces
the velocity--velocity term by its symmetrisation, which changes nothing; the
fourth collects all three metric derivatives over the common factor
$\tfrac{m}{2}\dot x^i\dot x^j$; the fifth is the definition of
$\Gamma_{k,ij}$. That is
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:ForcedGeodesicEquation}
m \left( g_{jk} \ddot{x}^j  + \Gamma_{k,ij} \dot{x}^i \dot{x}^j \right)
 = \mathfrak{F}_k
\end{equation}
and thus we reobtain the geodesic equation.

More precisely, Eq.~\ref{Eq:ForcedGeodesicEquation} is the \emph{forced}
geodesic equation, and it is the geodesic equation proper exactly when
$\mathfrak F_k=0$. Raising the index makes that visible: contract with the
inverse metric, $g^{lk}g_{kj}=\delta^l_j$, and write
$\Gamma^l_{ij}:=g^{lk}\Gamma_{k,ij}$, $\mathfrak F^l:=g^{lk}\mathfrak F_k$.
The contraction of Eq.~\ref{Eq:ForcedGeodesicEquation} is
\[
\begin{aligned}
 g^{lk}\mathfrak F_k
  &=m\bigl(g^{lk}g_{kj}\ddot x^j+g^{lk}\Gamma_{k,ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j\bigr)\\
  &=m\bigl(\delta^l_j\ddot x^j+\Gamma^l_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j\bigr)\\
  &=m\bigl(\ddot x^l+\Gamma^l_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j\bigr)=\mathfrak F^l.
\end{aligned}
\]
By the definition of the covariant acceleration, this is
$m\,\nabla_{\dot x}\dot x=\mathfrak F^\sharp$, Newton's second law written
covariantly, with $\nabla$ the Levi-Civita connection of $g$.
The $\Gamma$ terms are not forces. They are what the
derivative of the coordinate basis contributes, which is why they persist in
flat space in curvilinear coordinates --- where the curvature tensor vanishes
identically --- and disappear in Cartesian ones. The cylindrical example below
shows them arriving under the names centrifugal and Coriolis.

Two hypotheses deserve a second look. First, $m$ was pulled through $d/dt$
freely, so Eq.~\ref{Eq:MomentumInTermsOfMetricAndCoordinates} assumes $\dot
m=0$; with $m=m(t)$ an extra $\dot m\,g_{kj}\dot x^j$ appears and
Eq.~\ref{Eq:ForcedGeodesicEquation} fails as written. That is the same warning
issued later about taking $F=d(mv)/dt$ for a rocket. Second, the mass can
equally be absorbed into the metric, $G_{ij}:=m\,g_{ij}$, giving
$T=\tfrac12G_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j$; Christoffel symbols of the first kind are
linear in the metric, $\Gamma_{k,ij}[G]=m\,\Gamma_{k,ij}[g]$, and those of the
second kind are invariant under constant rescaling,
$\Gamma^l_{ij}[G]=\Gamma^l_{ij}[g]$, so nothing in
Eq.~\ref{Eq:ForcedGeodesicEquation} changes. A free particle follows the
geodesics of either metric; the mass only sets the scale at which a given force
bends them.

\subsubsection*{Notation from here on}
The split $\mathfrak F_k=f_k+F_k$ was bookkeeping. In the rocket sections that
follow no potential is carried explicitly, so $\mathfrak F=f$ there, and $f$
is written for the total force covector. Reinstating a
potential changes nothing, since only the sum enters
Eq.~\ref{Eq:EulerLagrangeEquationForm2}.

\subsection{Promotion and demotion: the musical isomorphisms}
A metric $g$ supplies --- and is exactly what supplies --- the identification
between the two spaces.

\begin{definition}
For a Riemannian metric $g$, define
$\flat:T_xM\to T^*_xM$ by $v^\flat=g(v,\cdot)$, so
$(v^\flat)_i=g_{ij}v^j$, and $\sharp:T^*_xM\to T_xM$ by
$(\alpha^\sharp)^i=g^{ij}\alpha_j$, where $g^{ij}g_{jk}=\delta^i_k$. The two are
mutually inverse.
\end{definition}

So one may always ``promote'' the force $1$-form to a vector field
$f^\sharp\in\mathfrak X(M)$, with $(f^\sharp)^i=g^{ij}f_j$, and Newton's second
law takes either the covector form $m(\nabla_{\dot x}\dot x)^\flat=f$ or the
vector form $m\nabla_{\dot x}\dot x=f^\sharp$. Nothing is lost --- but the
promotion is a choice that consumes the metric, and the two objects transform
differently under a change of coordinates.

In Cartesian coordinates on Euclidean $\R^3$, $g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}$ and the
components of $f$ and $f^\sharp$ are numerically equal. That coincidence is the
whole reason engineering mechanics can treat force as a vector without ever
being wrong. It stops being a coincidence in curvilinear coordinates. In
cylindrical coordinates $(r,\theta,z)$ on flat space,
\[
 g=\operatorname{diag}(1,\;r^2,\;1),\qquad
 f_\theta=r^2f^\theta,
\]
so the covariant, contravariant and \emph{physical} components
$f_{(\theta)}=f_\theta/r=r f^\theta$ are three different numbers. This is not
pedantry for turbomachinery: Hill and Peterson's angular-momentum balance,
$rF_\theta=\delta m\,\tfrac{d}{dt}(rc_\theta)$ \cite[Eq.~7.5]{hillpeterson},
carries exactly these $r$ weightings, and stating which component is meant is
what keeps a torque bookkeeping consistent.

\subsection{Impulse is the increment of a covector}
Write the impulse of $f$ over $[t_0,t_1]$ as
$J=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}f(t)\,dt$. There is an obstruction hiding in that
expression: $f(t)\in T^*_{x(t)}M$, and covectors at \emph{different} points lie
in different vector spaces, so the integrand cannot simply be summed. Making
$J$ well defined requires one of

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item a flat space with a globally parallel (inertial Cartesian) frame, so all
      fibres are canonically identified; or
\item a connection, integrating the parallel-transported covector; or
\item contraction with a covariantly constant vector field first, reducing the
      integrand to a scalar.
\end{itemize}

\noindent Rocket performance uses the first, and the third is what the scalar
total impulse actually is.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:impulse-momentum}
In Cartesian coordinates on Euclidean $\R^3$, $g$ is constant, hence
$\frac{\partial T}{\partial x^k}=0$ and the Euler--Lagrange equation reduces to
$\dot p_k=f_k$. Integrating,
$J=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}f\,dt=p(t_1)-p(t_0)$, an identity in $T^*\R^3$.
\end{proposition}

\noindent Every step of that is now available. With $g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}$
constant, every $\frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial x^k}$ vanishes, so
Eq.~\ref{Eq:PartialDerivativeOfPointParticleKineticEnergy} gives $\frac{\partial T}{\partial x^k}=0$, every $\Gamma_{k,ij}=0$, and
Eq.~\ref{Eq:EulerLagrangeEquationForm2} and
Eq.~\ref{Eq:ForcedGeodesicEquation} both collapse to
\[
 \dot p_k=f_k ,\qquad p_k=m\,\delta_{kj}\dot x^j ,
\]
whose components are numerically the familiar $m\dot x^k$. Integrating slot by
slot over $[t_0,t_1]$,
\[
 J_k=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}f_k\,dt=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\dot p_k\,dt
   =p_k(t_1)-p_k(t_0) ,
\]
and the hypothesis is what licenses reading this as a statement about
covectors rather than about three unrelated numbers: in an inertial Cartesian
frame every fibre $T^*_{x(t)}\R^3$ is canonically identified with one copy of
$(\R^3)^*$, so adding integrands taken at different points means something.
Without a specified identification of the fibres, the component integrals
still define numbers, but generally do not transform as a single covector
under a change of coordinates. A connection and transport to one reference
point provide a coordinate-independent comparison.

The failure is not exotic. It is already visible in cylindrical coordinates on
the same flat space.

\subsubsection*{The cylindrical case, explicitly}
Take $(r,\theta,z)$ with $g=\operatorname{diag}(1,r^2,1)$, so
$T=\tfrac12m(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2+\dot z^2)$. Then
Eq.~\ref{Eq:MomentumOfPointParticle} gives
\[
 p_r=m\dot r,\qquad p_\theta=mr^2\dot\theta,\qquad p_z=m\dot z,
\]
and, since $\frac{\partial g_{\theta\theta}}{\partial r}=2r$ is the only non-vanishing
derivative of the metric,
Eq.~\ref{Eq:PartialDerivativeOfPointParticleKineticEnergy} gives
\[
 \frac{\partial T}{\partial r}=\frac{m}{2}\,(2r)\,\dot\theta^2=mr\dot\theta^2,
 \qquad
 \frac{\partial T}{\partial\theta}=0,\qquad
 \frac{\partial T}{\partial z}=0 .
\]
Equation~\ref{Eq:EulerLagrangeEquationForm2} in the radial slot gives
$m\ddot r=f_r+mr\dot\theta^2$, i.e.\ $f_r=m(\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2)$ --- the
centrifugal term is the $\frac{\partial T}{\partial r}$ contribution, not a fictitious
force added by hand. In the angular slot $\frac{\partial T}{\partial \theta}=0$, so
\[
 \dot p_\theta=f_\theta
 \;\Longleftrightarrow\;
 \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(mr^2\dot\theta\bigr)=f_\theta ,
\]
and expanding gives $m(r^2\ddot\theta+2r\dot r\dot\theta)=f_\theta$, whose
$2r\dot r\dot\theta$ is the Coriolis term.

The forced geodesic form, Eq.~\ref{Eq:ForcedGeodesicEquation}, must reproduce
both, and does. From $\frac{\partial g_{\theta\theta}}{\partial r}=2r$ alone,
\[
 \Gamma_{r,\theta\theta}
 =\tfrac12\Bigl(2\frac{\partial g_{r\theta}}{\partial\theta}
   -\frac{\partial g_{\theta\theta}}{\partial r}\Bigr)=-r ,
 \qquad
 \Gamma_{\theta,r\theta}=\Gamma_{\theta,\theta r}
 =\tfrac12\frac{\partial g_{\theta\theta}}{\partial r}=r ,
\]
with every other $\Gamma_{k,ij}$ zero, so
Eq.~\ref{Eq:ForcedGeodesicEquation} reads
\[
\begin{aligned}
 k=r:&\quad m\bigl(\ddot r+\Gamma_{r,\theta\theta}\dot\theta^2\bigr)
   =m\bigl(\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2\bigr)=f_r,\\
 k=\theta:&\quad m\bigl(r^2\ddot\theta
   +2\Gamma_{\theta,r\theta}\dot r\dot\theta\bigr)
   =m\bigl(r^2\ddot\theta+2r\dot r\dot\theta\bigr)=f_\theta ,
\end{aligned}
\]
matching the two lines above term for term.

Note what the lower index bought: $p_\theta=mr^2\dot\theta$ is the angular
momentum and $f_\theta$ is the torque, both with an extra factor of $r$
relative to the physical components $c_\theta=r\dot\theta$ and
$f_{(\theta)}=f_\theta/r$. Hill and Peterson's
$rF_\theta=\delta m\,\tfrac{d}{dt}(rc_\theta)$
\cite[Eq.~7.5]{hillpeterson} is exactly $\dot p_\theta=f_\theta$ written in
physical components, since $r\,c_\theta=r^2\dot\theta=p_\theta/m$ and their
$rF_\theta$ is our $f_\theta$. The covariant components are the ones an
engineer already calls angular momentum and torque; the index position is
telling you which quantity you are holding.

Because $\frac{\partial T}{\partial x^k}\neq0$ here, the naive componentwise
$\int f\,dt=\Delta p$ is false in these coordinates.

\subsection{From the covector to Sutton's total impulse}
Sutton defines total impulse by
$I_t=\int_0^t F\,dt$ \cite[Eq.~2--1]{sutton}, where $F$ is a scalar. The
geometric content is the third bullet above: if $\hat e$ is a fixed unit
direction (the thrust axis) and $F=f(\hat e)$, then
\[
 I_t=\int_0^{t}f(\hat e)\,dt=J(\hat e),
\]
one component of the impulse covector. The evaluation $J(\hat e)$ needs no
metric once $\hat e$ is supplied, but normalising that vector does:
$\hat e=e/\sqrt{g(e,e)}$. Four assumptions separate this from the
general statement.

\begin{description}\itemsep2pt
\item[A1] Flat space, inertial Cartesian frame --- so $J$ exists
  (Prop.~\ref{prop:impulse-momentum}).
\item[A2] The thrust direction $\hat e$ is constant over $[0,t]$.
\item[A3] $F\ge0$, the thrust magnitude along that axis.
\item[A4] Start and stop transients are negligible, where Eq.~2--2 is used.
\end{description}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:scalar-bound}
$\lVert J\rVert\le\int_0^t\lVert f\rVert\,dt$, using the Euclidean dual norm.
Equality holds exactly when the nonzero force covectors lie on one common
ray almost everywhere in time. Thus a change of thrust direction during
nonzero thrust, on time sets with nonzero impulse contribution, makes the
scalar integral of thrust magnitude strictly exceed $\lVert J\rVert$.
Direction changes confined to zero thrust do not affect equality.
\end{proposition}

\noindent To see both the inequality and its equality condition, suppose first
that $J\ne0$ and set $u=J^\sharp/\lVert J\rVert$. Then $g(u,u)=1$, and
\[
 \lVert J\rVert=J(u)
 =\int_0^t f(u)\,dt
 \le\int_0^t\lVert f\rVert\lVert u\rVert\,dt
 =\int_0^t\lVert f\rVert\,dt.
\]
The inequality is the pointwise Cauchy--Schwarz inequality. Equality of the
integrals requires $\lVert f\rVert-f(u)=0$ almost everywhere, since this
integrand is nonnegative. Equality in the pointwise inequality then gives
$f=\lambda(t)u^\flat$, $\lambda(t)\ge0$, almost everywhere. Conversely,
such an $f$ gives $J=(\int_0^t\lambda\,dt)u^\flat$, so the norms are equal.
If $J=0$, equality requires $\int_0^t\lVert f\rVert\,dt=0$, hence $f=0$
almost everywhere. These observations include the zero-thrust case.

The scalar total impulse bounds the magnitude of the thrust impulse.
Converting either quantity into a vehicle $\Delta v$ also requires the mass
history and the vehicle's force and momentum balance.

Sutton's Eq.~2--2, $I_t=Ft$, follows by additionally holding $F$ constant: with
$F$ constant the integral $\int_0^tF\,dt$ is just $Ft$, and A4 is what allows
the start and stop transients --- during which $F$ is emphatically not constant
--- to be left out of the integral rather than modelled. For specific impulse Sutton writes
\[
 I_s=\frac{\int_0^t F\,dt}{g_0\int_0^t\dot m\,dt}
 \qquad\text{\cite[Eq.~2--3]{sutton}},
\]
a ratio of two integrals. It is worth being explicit about what average that is.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:weighted-mean}
Let $\dot m(t)>0$ denote the propellant consumption rate during the averaging
interval; it is the negative of the vehicle mass derivative for pure
propellant loss. With the instantaneous value $I_s(t)=F/(\dot m g_0)$,
\[
 \frac{\int F\,dt}{g_0\int\dot m\,dt}
 =\frac{\int I_s(t)\,\dot m\,dt}{\int \dot m\,dt},
\]
the \emph{mass-flow-weighted} mean of $I_s$. It equals the time average exactly
when the time covariance of $I_s$ and $\dot m$ vanishes. Constant $\dot m$ is
sufficient, but is not necessary.
\end{proposition}

\noindent Explicitly, for an interval of length $\tau>0$, define
\[
 \langle h\rangle_t:=\frac1\tau\int_0^\tau h(t)\,dt,\qquad
 \operatorname{Cov}_t(I_s,\dot m)
 :=\langle I_s\dot m\rangle_t-\langle I_s\rangle_t\langle\dot m\rangle_t.
\]
Then the difference of the two means is
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:SpecificImpulseMeanDifference}
\begin{aligned}
 \frac{\int_0^\tau I_s\dot m\,dt}{\int_0^\tau\dot m\,dt}
      -\langle I_s\rangle_t
  &=\frac{\langle I_s\dot m\rangle_t}{\langle\dot m\rangle_t}
      -\langle I_s\rangle_t\\
  &=\frac{\langle I_s\dot m\rangle_t
          -\langle I_s\rangle_t\langle\dot m\rangle_t}
         {\langle\dot m\rangle_t}
   =\frac{\operatorname{Cov}_t(I_s,\dot m)}{\langle\dot m\rangle_t}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The denominator is positive. As a counterexample to necessity of constant
mass flow, take $I_s(t)=I_0$ and any positive variable $\dot m(t)$. Both means
then equal $I_0$, since
$\int I_0\dot m\,dt/\int\dot m\,dt=I_0$ and
$\tau^{-1}\int_0^\tau I_0\,dt=I_0$.

\noindent Sutton's Eq.~2--5, $I_s=F/(\dot m g_0)$, is then the constant-$F$,
constant-$\dot m$ case, and Eq.~2--4, $I_s=I_t/(m_pg_0)$, follows from
$\int\dot m\,dt=m_p$.

Two further points of hygiene. First, $g_0$ is a \emph{defined} constant,
$9.80665\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$, not the local gravitational acceleration; Sutton says
so, and notes that in orbit $m_pg_0$ ``does not represent the weight''
\cite[Section 2.1]{sutton}. Consequently $I_s$ measured in seconds is not a
duration --- it is a velocity divided by a conventional constant, and the unit
is an artefact of that convention.

Second, and this is where the metric re-enters, Sutton's effective exhaust
velocity
\[
 c=I_sg_0=F/\dot m \qquad\text{\cite[Eq.~2--6]{sutton}}
\]
divides a force by a mass flow rate and calls the result a velocity. Force is a
covector and velocity is a vector, so the equality is really
$c\,\hat e=(f/\dot m)^\sharp$: the identification is the musical isomorphism,
performed silently. In Cartesian components on Euclidean space the two sides
have equal numbers, so nothing goes wrong. The metric enters through both
the musical isomorphism and the unit normalisation of $\hat e$. In fact,
for thrust aligned with a unit $\hat e$, $f=F\hat e^\flat$ and
\[
 (f/\dot m)^\sharp
 =\frac{F}{\dot m}(\hat e^\flat)^\sharp
 =\frac{F}{\dot m}\hat e=c\hat e.
\]
The pairing operation itself is metric-free; scalar magnitudes and unit
directions used in both $I_t$ and $c$ carry metric information.

\subsection{Where the thrust covector comes from}
$f$ in the vehicle equation of motion is not primitive either: it is the
resultant of surface tractions --- pressure and viscous --- over the wetted
boundary. Traction is naturally a covector-valued $2$-form, the object Frankel
uses for stress and which pairs with a deformation $1$-form to give a scalar
$3$-form of work \cite[Section on Elasticity and Stresses]{frankel}. Sutton's
momentum-flux expression for thrust is a consequence of a control-volume
balance, not a definition of force, and the rocket's variable mass means the
vehicle-only $F=d(mv)/dt$ is \emph{not} a correct starting point. That
derivation belongs with Sutton Section 2.2 and is not attempted here.

\subsection{Notation: the symbol \texorpdfstring{$c$}{c}}
Reading Sutton and Hill and Peterson together creates a live collision.

\begin{description}\itemsep2pt
\item[Sutton] $c$ is the effective exhaust velocity \cite[Eq.~2--6]{sutton};
  $c^*$ is characteristic velocity. Sutton notes the Russian literature uses
  $c$ in place of $I_s$.
\item[Hill and Peterson] $c$ (and $\mathbf c$) is the \emph{absolute fluid
  velocity}, with components $c_r,c_\theta,c_z$ throughout the turbomachinery
  chapters; $c$ also denotes duct circumference, specific heat of a liquid and
  the speed of light. Speed of sound is $a$, and $c^*$ is again characteristic
  velocity \cite[Appendix X]{hillpeterson}.
\end{description}

\noindent So $c$ \emph{is} the propulsion convention for effective exhaust
velocity, and it is worth keeping rather than inventing a private symbol. The
collision is with Hill and Peterson's fluid velocity, not with the speed of
sound --- these notes already write $a$ for the latter, as in the nozzle topic.
The rule adopted here: $c$ denotes effective exhaust velocity in
rocket-performance contexts; subscripted $c_r,c_\theta,c_z$ denote absolute
fluid velocity components in turbomachinery contexts, following Hill and
Peterson; and where both appear in one derivation, the exhaust velocity is
written $v_e$ and said to equal $c$.

\subsection{Scope of what is claimed}
Propositions~\ref{prop:momentum-covector}--\ref{prop:weighted-mean} are
mathematical identities under their stated hypotheses, verifiable by direct
computation; they are not physical claims. A1--A4 are modelling assumptions
about a particular burn, and whether they hold is an engineering question about
the vehicle, not something the geometry decides. No numerical or symbolic study
accompanies this topic yet, so no executable evidence is claimed here; the
natural first check is Proposition~\ref{prop:scalar-bound} on a slewing burn,
comparing $\int\lVert f\rVert\,dt$ against $\lVert\int f\,dt\rVert$.

\subsection{An erratum, and why it is the relevant kind}
Sutton's worked Example~2--1 (printed p.~31 / PDF p.~55) prints
\[
 F=I_sg_0=23.3\times2354.4=54{,}936\ \mathrm{N},
\]
which was checked here against the source PDF's own text layer, not merely
against OCR. Two defects sit in that line. First, $I_sg_0=2354.4\
\mathrm{m/s}$ is the effective exhaust velocity $c$ --- the book computes it as
$c$ one sentence earlier --- so the equation sets a force equal to a velocity.
Eq.~2--6 rearranges to $F=\dot mc=\dot mI_sg_0$; the factor $\dot m$ is
missing. Second, the displayed multiplicand does not reproduce the stated
result: $23.3\times2354.4=54{,}857.5$, whereas the printed $54{,}936$ follows
from the unrounded $\dot m=70/3$, since $2354.4\times70/3=54{,}936$ exactly.

Every downstream quantity in the example is nevertheless correct, including
$I_t=164{,}808\ \mathrm{N\,s}$ --- independently confirmed by
$I_sm_pg_0=240\times70\times9.81$ --- and the final acceleration
$422.58\ \mathrm{m/s^2}=43.08\,g_0$. Only the symbolic statement is wrong, and
it survives into the ninth edition.

The missing mass-flow factor is already visible to dimensional analysis,
whether force is represented as a vector or a covector:
\[
 [I_sg_0]=\mathrm{s}\,\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s^2}}
        =\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}},\qquad
 [\dot m I_sg_0]=\frac{\mathrm{kg}}{\mathrm{s}}
                  \frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}}
                =\frac{\mathrm{kg\,m}}{\mathrm{s^2}}=\mathrm{N}.
\]
The distinction between $f\in T^*M$ and
$c\,\hat e=(f/\dot m)^\sharp\in TM$ supplies a separate geometric check.
The metric converts tensor type after division by the mass-flow rate; it
does not supply the missing physical units.
