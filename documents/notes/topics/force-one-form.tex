\topic{force-one-form}{Force as a one-form, and what that makes impulse}

Reading anchor: Sutton and Biblarz, ninth edition, Section 2.1, printed
p.~26 / PDF p.~50 \cite{sutton}; Frankel, Overview Section O.f, ``Line
Integrals'', printed p.~xxxix \cite{frankel}; Hill and Peterson, Sections 1.2
and 7.2, printed pp.~4 and 277 \cite{hillpeterson}. Claim: force is naturally
a covector, impulse inherits that status, and Sutton's Eqs.~2--1 through
2--6 are flat-space specialisations of the force--displacement pairing. The
pairing needs no metric. Unit directions, force magnitudes, and turning a
covector into a velocity vector all do.

\subsection{Work is a pairing, not a dot product}
Let $M$ be a smooth manifold, the configuration space of the mechanical
system. At each $x\in M$ the tangent space $T_xM$ holds velocities and the
cotangent space $T^*_xM$ holds linear functionals on them. The evaluation
$T^*_xM\times T_xM\to\R$, $(\alpha,v)\mapsto\alpha(v)$, is defined by the
manifold structure alone; it requires \emph{no} metric.

A position-dependent force is specified by its pairing with displacements.
For a curve $C:[a,b]\to M$, $t\mapsto x(t)$,
\[
 W=\int_C f=\int_a^b f\bigl(\dot x(t)\bigr)\,dt
  =\int_a^b f_k\bigl(x(t)\bigr)\frac{dx^k}{dt}\,dt ,
\]
the integral of the pullback $C^*f$ over $[a,b]$. The integrand is a
$1$-form $f=f_k\,dx^k\in\Omega^1(M)$, and $W$ is independent of the
parameterisation at fixed orientation. Frankel: the integrand of a line
integral is naturally a $1$-form, and the work integral ``does not require a
metric'' \cite[Section O.f]{frankel}.

A force that also depends on velocity and time, $f(x,v,t)\in T_x^*M$, is a
covector at $x$ parametrised by $(v,t)$, not a single one-form on $M$. Its
work along the actual motion,
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:VelocityDependentForceWork}
 W=\int_a^b f_k(x(t),\dot x(t),t)\dot x^k(t)\,dt,
\end{equation}
depends on the timing. For example, the damper $f=-b\dot x\,dx$ traversing
a length $\ell$ uniformly in time $\tau$ does work $W=-b\ell^2/\tau$.

The familiar $W=\int\mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r$ presupposes a metric $g$, for
the dot product, and usually Cartesian components, where $g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}$
hides the distinction. The rocket problem has both, which is why the
shortcut is safe there.

\subsection{Why the index is down: the Legendre transform}
The covector status of force follows from the variational formulation,
which uses no metric. Here we take $L=T(x,\dot x)-V(x)$. The metric enters
only when a particular $T$ is chosen, in the next subsection.

For the general form of the Lagrangian that only depends upon $x,\dot{x}$,
and assuming that $V=V(x)$ (i.e.\ potential $V$ has no dependency on
$\dot{x}$), then
\[
L = L(x,\dot{x}) = T(x,\dot{x}) - V(x)
\]
Also recall that momentum $p_k$ is defined in terms of the Lagrangian as
follows:
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:MomentumDefinitionFromLagrangian}
p_k := \frac{\partial L}{ \partial \dot{x}^k}
\end{equation}

So
\begin{align}
& \frac{\partial L }{ \partial x^i}
  = \frac{\partial T}{ \partial x^i} - \frac{ \partial V}{\partial x^i}
  \label{Eq:LagrangianFormPart2} \\
& \frac{\partial L }{ \partial \dot{x}^i}
  = \frac{\partial T}{ \partial \dot{x}^i}
  \label{Eq:LagrangianFormPart0} \\
& \Longrightarrow \frac{d}{dt} \frac{\partial L }{ \partial \dot{x}^i}
  = \frac{ \partial^2 T}{ \partial x^j \partial \dot{x}^i } \dot{x}^j
  + \frac{ \partial^2 T}{ \partial \dot{x}^j \partial \dot{x}^i } \ddot{x}^j
  = \dot{p}_i \label{Eq:LagrangianFormPart1}
\end{align}

\noindent where the last equality was obtained using
Eq.~\ref{Eq:MomentumDefinitionFromLagrangian}, the definition of momentum
$p_i$. The time derivative is the chain rule on $TM$: both slots of
$\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot x^i}(x,\dot x)$ move along the trajectory,
with $dx^j/dt=\dot x^j$ and $d\dot x^j/dt=\ddot x^j$. The first term is
present whenever $\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot x^i}$ depends on position.

If we consider the Euler--Lagrange equation, which could take the following
forms:
\[
\begin{aligned}
& \frac{d}{dt} \frac{\partial L }{ \partial \dot{x}^k}
  - \frac{\partial L }{ \partial x^k} = 0 \text{ or } \\
& \frac{d}{dt} \frac{\partial L }{ \partial \dot{x}^k}
  - \frac{\partial L }{ \partial x^k} = f_k
\end{aligned}
\]
where $f_k$ is some external force (or forces) applied on the total system
that has not been written into $L$, then from the Euler--Lagrange equation
and Eqns.~\ref{Eq:LagrangianFormPart1}, \ref{Eq:LagrangianFormPart2}, we get
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}
   -\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}
 &=\dot p_k-\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}
 &&\text{by Eq.~\ref{Eq:LagrangianFormPart1}}\\
 &=\dot p_k-\frac{\partial T}{\partial x^k}
   +\frac{\partial V}{\partial x^k}
 &&\text{by Eq.~\ref{Eq:LagrangianFormPart2}}\\
 &=\dot p_k-\frac{\partial T}{\partial x^k}-F_k ,
\end{aligned}
\]
where $F_k := -\frac{\partial V}{\partial x^k}$ (i.e.\ the negative of the
gradient of a conservative potential is a conservative force due to the
potential). We can combine the force due to the conservative potential and
the ``external'' force that's not necessarily conservative into one force
term:
\[
\mathfrak{F}_k \equiv f_k + F_k
\]
so that
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:EulerLagrangeEquationForm2}
	\dot{p}_k - \frac{\partial T}{ \partial x^k} = \mathfrak{F}_k
\end{equation}

The momentum $p_k=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}$ is the image of the
fibre derivative $\mathbb FL:TM\to T^*M$, and
$F_k=-\frac{\partial V}{\partial x^k}$ are the components of $-dV$. Both are
covectors. But $\dot p_k$ and $\frac{\partial T}{\partial x^k}$ separately are
generally \emph{not} covector components; only their difference is.

To see this, let $x=x(q)$ be a time-independent coordinate change, and
define
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:CoordinateJacobianForEL}
\begin{gathered}
 A^i{}_a:=\frac{\partial x^i}{\partial q^a},\qquad
 \dot x^i=A^i{}_a\dot q^a,\\
 \widetilde L(q,\dot q):=L\bigl(x(q),A(q)\dot q\bigr).
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
At fixed $q$, the chain rule gives
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:MomentumCovectorTransformation}
 \widetilde p_a:=\frac{\partial\widetilde L}{\partial\dot q^a}
 =\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^i}
   \frac{\partial\dot x^i}{\partial\dot q^a}
 =p_i A^i{}_a .
\end{equation}
At fixed $\dot q$, however, $\dot x$ is not fixed:
$\frac{\partial\dot x^i}{\partial q^a}\big|_{\dot q}
=\frac{\partial A^i{}_b}{\partial q^a}\dot q^b$. Hence
\begin{align}
 \frac{d\widetilde p_a}{dt}
 &=A^i{}_a\dot p_i+p_i\frac{\partial A^i{}_a}{\partial q^b}\dot q^b,
 \label{Eq:MomentumDerivativeTransformation}\\
 \frac{\partial\widetilde L}{\partial q^a}
 &=A^i{}_a\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^i}
   +p_i\frac{\partial A^i{}_b}{\partial q^a}\dot q^b.
 \label{Eq:PositionDerivativeTransformation}
\end{align}
Mixed partials of the coordinate map commute,
$\frac{\partial A^i{}_a}{\partial q^b}
=\frac{\partial^2x^i}{\partial q^b\partial q^a}
=\frac{\partial A^i{}_b}{\partial q^a}$, so subtracting
Eq.~\ref{Eq:PositionDerivativeTransformation} from
Eq.~\ref{Eq:MomentumDerivativeTransformation} cancels the extra terms:
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:ELCovectorTransformation}
 \frac{d\widetilde p_a}{dt}-\frac{\partial\widetilde L}{\partial q^a}
 =A^i{}_a\left(\dot p_i-\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^i}\right).
\end{equation}
This is the covector law \cite[Section 2.2]{tongdynamics}. Each term
separately picks up $p_i\frac{\partial A^i{}_a}{\partial q^b}\dot q^b$.
For $L=T-V(x)$ the same holds with $T$ in place of $L$:
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:KineticELCovectorTransformation}
 \frac{d\widetilde p_a}{dt}-\frac{\partial\widetilde T}{\partial q^a}
 =A^i{}_a\left(\dot p_i-\frac{\partial T}{\partial x^i}\right).
\end{equation}
Note that $-dV$ requires no metric, while the gradient $(dV)^\sharp$ does.

\subsubsection*{The homogeneous form, and the forced form}
Fix endpoints $x(t_0)=x_0$, $x(t_1)=x_1$, let
$S[x]=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}L(x,\dot x)\,dt$, and vary
$x_\varepsilon^k=x^k+\varepsilon\eta^k$ with $\eta(t_0)=\eta(t_1)=0$.
Integrating the $\dot\eta$ term by parts,
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\Big|_{\varepsilon=0}S[x_\varepsilon]
 &=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\Bigl(\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}\eta^k
   +\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}\dot\eta^k\Bigr)dt\\
 &=\Bigl[\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}\eta^k\Bigr]_{t_0}^{t_1}
   -\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\Bigl(\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}
   -\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}\Bigr)\eta^k\,dt .
\end{aligned}
\]
The bracket vanishes at the fixed endpoints. Stationarity for every $\eta$
and the fundamental lemma give
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:EulerLagrangeHomogeneous}
 \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}
   -\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}=0 .
\end{equation}
The zero does not say ``no force''. It says no covector is left over once
everything has been put into $L$: a body falling under gravity carries its
force in $V$. The two ways of carrying a potential force give the same
equation. With $L_1=T$ and right side $-\frac{\partial V}{\partial x^k}$,
or with $L_2=T-V$ and right side $0$, expanding the second
(with $\frac{\partial V}{\partial\dot x^k}=0$) gives the first rearranged. So
the split $\mathfrak F_k=f_k+F_k$ is the modeller's bookkeeping; only the
total is fixed by the problem.

For forces kept outside $L$, the Lagrange--d'Alembert principle adds their
virtual work to the first variation:
\[
 \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\Bigl(\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}\eta^k
  +\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}\dot\eta^k
  +f_k\eta^k\Bigr)dt=0
 \quad\text{for all }\eta\text{ with }\eta(t_0)=\eta(t_1)=0 .
\]
The same integration by parts gives
$\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\bigl(\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}-\dot p_k+f_k\bigr)
\eta^k\,dt=0$, hence
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:EulerLagrangeForced}
 \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}
 -\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}=f_k .
\end{equation}
The term $f_k\eta^k$ is virtual work, a covector paired with a displacement.
This is the second, independent reason the index on $f$ is down: $f$ is
\emph{defined} by what it does to a virtual displacement. It also fixes how
a generalised force transforms. For particles with ambient force components
$F^{(a)}_i$ at positions $r_{(a)}(q)$,
\[
 \delta W=\sum_a F^{(a)}_i\,\delta r^i_{(a)}
  =\sum_a F^{(a)}_i\frac{\partial r^i_{(a)}}{\partial q^k}\,\delta q^k
  =:Q_k\,\delta q^k ,
\]
and $\frac{\partial r^i}{\partial q^k}$ is the Jacobian of the covector law.
A torque appearing as the $\theta$ slot of a force covector, with an extra
factor of $r$, is this formula.

\paragraph{Energy.} Define $E:=p_k\dot x^k-L$. If $T$ is homogeneous of
degree two in velocity, $T(x,\lambda\dot x)=\lambda^2T(x,\dot x)$, then
differentiating at $\lambda=1$ gives
$\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot x^k}\dot x^k=2T$, so $E=2T-(T-V)=T+V$.
Along any solution,
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \frac{dE}{dt}
 &=\Bigl(\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}\Bigr)\dot x^k
   +\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}\ddot x^k
   -\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}\dot x^k
   -\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}\ddot x^k\\
 &=\Bigl(\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^k}
   -\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}\Bigr)\dot x^k
  =f_k\dot x^k .
\end{aligned}
\]
The power $f_k\dot x^k$ may be zero even when $f\ne0$.

\paragraph{Momentum.} Let $x_\varepsilon^k=x^k+\varepsilon\xi^k(x)
+O(\varepsilon^2)$ be a time-independent transformation, with induced
velocity variation $\frac{\partial\xi^k}{\partial x^j}\dot x^j$. Strict
invariance of $L$ means
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:StrictSymmetryCondition}
 0=\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}\xi^k
   +p_k\frac{\partial\xi^k}{\partial x^j}\dot x^j .
\end{equation}
Then, by Eq.~\ref{Eq:EulerLagrangeForced} and
Eq.~\ref{Eq:StrictSymmetryCondition},
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:ForcedSymmetryBalance}
\begin{aligned}
 \frac{d}{dt}(p_k\xi^k)
 &=\dot p_k\xi^k+p_k\frac{\partial\xi^k}{\partial x^j}\dot x^j\\
 &=\Bigl(\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^k}+f_k\Bigr)\xi^k
   +p_k\frac{\partial\xi^k}{\partial x^j}\dot x^j
 =f_k\xi^k .
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
So $p_k\xi^k$ is conserved when the force has zero pairing with the symmetry
\cite{deleonforced}.

\paragraph{Symplectic form.} For a regular $L$ (nonsingular velocity
Hessian $\frac{\partial^2L}{\partial\dot x^i\partial\dot x^j}$), solve
$p_i=\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot x^i}$ locally for $\dot x=v(x,p,t)$ and set
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:LocalHamiltonianDefinition}
 H(x,p,t):=p_iv^i-L(x,v,t).
\end{equation}
At fixed $t$, $dH=v^i\,dp_i-\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^i}\,dx^i$, since the
$dv^i$ terms cancel by $p_i=\frac{\partial L}{\partial v^i}$. With
Eq.~\ref{Eq:EulerLagrangeForced},
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:ForcedHamiltonEquations}
 \dot x^i=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i},\qquad
 \dot p_i=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial x^i}+f_i .
\end{equation}
Let $\omega=dx^i\wedge dp_i$, the canonical form on $T^*M$; its definition
does not involve $f$. For the vector field $X$ of
Eq.~\ref{Eq:ForcedHamiltonEquations},
$\iota_X\omega=X(x^i)\,dp_i-X(p_i)\,dx^i=dH-f_i\,dx^i$. Cartan's formula,
with $d\omega=0$, then gives
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:ForcedSymplecticBalance}
 \mathcal L_X\omega=d(\iota_X\omega)=-d(f_i\,dx^i).
\end{equation}
The flow preserves $\omega$ exactly when $f_i\,dx^i$ is closed on phase
space. A spring kept on the right, $f=-\kappa x$, gives
$-d(-\kappa x\,dx)=\kappa\,dx\wedge dx=0$: a nonzero applied force can still
give a symplectic flow, and here also conserves $p^2/2m+\kappa x^2/2$.
A damper $f=-\gamma p$ gives
$\mathcal L_X\omega=-\gamma\,dx\wedge dp\ne0$.

\subsubsection*{Which forces may be moved into the Lagrangian}
\begin{description}\itemsep3pt
\item[Conservative means exact.] A force one-form is conservative when
  $f=-dV$ for some $V(x)$; equivalently, its work depends only on the
  endpoints. Closedness,
  $\frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x^j}=\frac{\partial f_j}{\partial x^i}$, is
  equivalent only on a simply connected domain (Poincar\'e's lemma). On the
  punctured plane, the closed one-form written $d\theta$ is not exact and
  does net work around a loop.
\item[Time dependence.] If $L=L(x,\dot x,t)$, the energy calculation gains
  one term:
  \begin{equation}\label{Eq:ForcedTimeDependentEnergyBalance}
   \frac{dE}{dt}=f_k\dot x^k-\frac{\partial L}{\partial t}.
  \end{equation}
  So $V(x,t)$ is spatially exact at each instant, but $T+V$ is not
  conserved in general.
\item[Velocity dependence.] A force derives from a generalised potential
  $U(x,\dot x,t)$ when
  \begin{equation}\label{Eq:GeneralisedPotentialForce}
   \mathfrak F_k=\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial U}{\partial\dot x^k}
     -\frac{\partial U}{\partial x^k},
  \end{equation}
  and then $L=T-U$ restores the homogeneous form. The canonical momentum
  shifts away from the kinetic momentum
  $\pi_k:=\frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot x^k}$:
  \begin{equation}\label{Eq:CanonicalAndKineticMomentum}
   p_k=\pi_k-\frac{\partial U}{\partial\dot x^k},
   \qquad
   \dot\pi_k-\frac{\partial T}{\partial x^k}=\mathfrak F_k .
  \end{equation}
  The second equation is the homogeneous Euler--Lagrange equation for $T-U$
  with Eq.~\ref{Eq:GeneralisedPotentialForce} substituted.
  For the Lorentz force, $U=q\varphi-qA_i\dot x^i$, so
  $\frac{\partial U}{\partial\dot x^k}=-qA_k$ and
  $p_k=m\,g_{kj}\dot x^j+qA_k$. Substituting into
  Eq.~\ref{Eq:GeneralisedPotentialForce} and expanding $dA_k/dt$ along the
  path,
  \begin{equation}\label{Eq:LorentzForceFromGeneralisedPotential}
  \begin{aligned}
   \mathfrak F_k
    &=-q\Bigl(\frac{\partial A_k}{\partial t}
          +\frac{\partial A_k}{\partial x^j}\dot x^j\Bigr)
      -q\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x^k}
      +q\frac{\partial A_j}{\partial x^k}\dot x^j\\
    &=q\Bigl(-\frac{\partial A_k}{\partial t}
             -\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x^k}\Bigr)
      +qB_{kj}\dot x^j,
    \quad
    B_{kj}:=\frac{\partial A_j}{\partial x^k}-\frac{\partial A_k}{\partial x^j}.
  \end{aligned}
  \end{equation}
  The magnetic power $qB_{kj}\dot x^j\dot x^k$ vanishes because $B$ is
  antisymmetric, so this force does no work although it is not of the form
  $-dV(x)$.
\item[Rayleigh dissipation.] A linear damper $f_k=-C_{kj}\dot x^j$, with
  $C$ symmetric positive semi-definite, is kept on the right through
  $\mathcal R=\tfrac12C_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j$:
  \begin{equation}\label{Eq:RayleighForceAndPower}
   f_k=-\frac{\partial\mathcal R}{\partial\dot x^k}=-C_{kj}\dot x^j,
   \qquad
   f_k\dot x^k=-2\mathcal R\le0 .
  \end{equation}
  It cannot be moved into a generalised potential with $T$ fixed.
  Eq.~\ref{Eq:GeneralisedPotentialForce} must hold for every acceleration,
  so $\frac{\partial^2U}{\partial\dot x^k\partial\dot x^j}=0$ and
  $U=a_j(x,t)\dot x^j+b(x,t)$. Its velocity-linear force is then
  $\bigl(\frac{\partial a_k}{\partial x^j}-\frac{\partial a_j}{\partial x^k}
  \bigr)\dot x^j$, whose coefficient is antisymmetric in $k,j$. It equals the
  symmetric $-C_{kj}$ only if $C=0$. A different Lagrangian can still give
  the same trajectories: for constant $m$ and rate $\gamma$,
  $L_d=e^{\gamma t}\bigl(\tfrac12m\dot x^2-V(x)\bigr)$ yields
  $e^{\gamma t}(m\ddot x+m\gamma\dot x+V'(x))=0$ \cite{leonegourieux}. Its
  canonical momentum $e^{\gamma t}m\dot x$ is not the mechanical one.
\item[Thrust stays on the right.] Rocket thrust is set by propellant flow,
  exhaust conditions and control inputs, so it is modelled as an applied
  force. A prescribed uniform $f_k(t)$ could be written as
  $U=-f_k(t)x^k$, but that does not supply the open-system momentum balance
  of a vehicle that loses mass. Section~\ref{topic:vehicle-acceleration}
  derives that balance.
\end{description}

\noindent So $f_k$ is whatever has not been put into $L$. The split is a
modelling choice; the total $\mathfrak F_k$ and its covector character are
not.

\subsection{A point particle: kinetic energy built from a metric}
We now return to $L=T(x,\dot x)-V(x)$. Two hypotheses are used below: the
metric $g$ is Riemannian with components $g_{ij}(x)$ depending on position
only, and the mass $m$ is constant, so that it passes through $d/dt$.

Indeed, let's show how these equations are fulfilled for the kinetic energy
term $T$ having the following form:
\[
T = \frac{1}{2} m g_{ij} \dot{x}^i \dot{x}^j
\]
For a metric $g_{ij} = g_{ij}(x)$ dependent upon coordinates $x$, and
symmetric in $i,j$, then
\begin{align}
& \frac{ \partial T}{\partial x^k}
  = \frac{m}{2} \left( \frac{ \partial g_{ij}}{ \partial x^k}
    \dot{x}^i \dot{x}^j \right)
  \label{Eq:PartialDerivativeOfPointParticleKineticEnergy} \\
& \frac{ \partial T}{ \partial \dot{x}^k} = m g_{kj} \dot{x}^j
  \label{Eq:MomentumOfPointParticle}
\end{align}
In Eq.~\ref{Eq:PartialDerivativeOfPointParticleKineticEnergy} only $g_{ij}$
depends on $x$; the fibre coordinates $\dot x^i$ are held fixed. For
Eq.~\ref{Eq:MomentumOfPointParticle}, use the product rule and
$\frac{\partial\dot x^i}{\partial\dot x^k}=\delta^i_k$:
\[
 \frac{\partial T}{\partial\dot x^k}
 =\frac{m}{2}\,g_{ij}\bigl(\delta^i_k\dot x^j+\dot x^i\delta^j_k\bigr)
 =\frac{m}{2}\bigl(g_{kj}\dot x^j+g_{ik}\dot x^i\bigr)
 =m\,g_{kj}\dot x^j ,
\]
renaming $i\to j$ and using $g_{jk}=g_{kj}$.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:momentum-covector}
For $T=\tfrac12m\,g_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j$ and $V=V(x)$, the momentum
conjugate to $x^k$ is
$p_k=\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot x^k}=\frac{\partial T}{\partial \dot x^k}
=m\,g_{kj}\dot x^j$; that is, $p=m\,v^\flat$.
\end{proposition}

\noindent This is Eq.~\ref{Eq:MomentumOfPointParticle} with
Eqs.~\ref{Eq:MomentumDefinitionFromLagrangian} and
\ref{Eq:LagrangianFormPart0}. Frankel: $p$ ``is $m$ times the covariant
version of the velocity vector'' \cite[Section O.f]{frankel}. The fibre
derivative gives a covector for any $L$; for this $L$ the metric in $T$
determines the map $v\mapsto m\,v^\flat$.

Differentiating Eq.~\ref{Eq:MomentumOfPointParticle} once more,
\[
\Longrightarrow \quad
\begin{aligned}
& \frac{ \partial^2 T}{ \partial \dot{x}^i \partial x^k}
  = m \left( \frac{\partial g_{ij} }{ \partial x^k}  \dot{x}^j \right)\\
& \frac{ \partial^2 T}{ \partial \dot{x}^j \partial \dot{x}^k } = m g_{jk}
\end{aligned}
\]
then from Eq.~\ref{Eq:LagrangianFormPart1} (with $i\to k$, and
$g_{ki}=g_{ik}$), we get
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:MomentumInTermsOfMetricAndCoordinates}
\dot{p}_k = m \left( \frac{\partial g_{ik} }{ \partial x^j}
  \dot{x}^j \dot{x}^i + g_{kj} \ddot{x}^j \right)
\end{equation}

Because $g_{kj}$ is symmetric in $k,j$ then we can write
\[
\frac{ \partial g_{kj}}{\partial x^i} \dot{x}^i \dot{x}^j
 = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{ \partial g_{kj}}{ \partial x^i}
   + \frac{ \partial g_{ki}}{\partial x^j} \right) \dot{x}^i \dot{x}^j
\]
The symmetry actually used is that of $\dot x^i\dot x^j$: renaming the
dummies $i\leftrightarrow j$ gives
$\frac{\partial g_{kj}}{\partial x^i}\dot x^i\dot x^j
=\frac{\partial g_{ki}}{\partial x^j}\dot x^i\dot x^j$, and the identity is the
average of the two sides.

\noindent And recall the definition of this form of the Christoffel symbol:
\[
	\Gamma_{k, ij} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{ \partial g_{kj}}{ \partial x^i}
   + \frac{\partial g_{ki} }{ \partial x^j}
   - \frac{ \partial g_{ij} }{ \partial x^k} \right)
\]
then combining the terms in
Eq.~\ref{Eq:PartialDerivativeOfPointParticleKineticEnergy},
Eq.~\ref{Eq:MomentumInTermsOfMetricAndCoordinates}, definition of
$\Gamma_{k,ij}$ into Eq.~\ref{Eq:EulerLagrangeEquationForm2}, we get
\[
\begin{aligned}
 \mathfrak F_k
 &=\dot p_k-\frac{\partial T}{\partial x^k}\\
 &=m\Bigl(\frac{\partial g_{ki}}{\partial x^j}\dot x^i\dot x^j
   +g_{kj}\ddot x^j\Bigr)
   -\frac{m}{2}\frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial x^k}\dot x^i\dot x^j\\
 &=m\,g_{kj}\ddot x^j
   +\frac{m}{2}\Bigl(\frac{\partial g_{kj}}{\partial x^i}
     +\frac{\partial g_{ki}}{\partial x^j}
     -\frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial x^k}\Bigr)\dot x^i\dot x^j\\
 &=m\bigl(g_{kj}\ddot x^j+\Gamma_{k,ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j\bigr),
\end{aligned}
\]
the third line by the symmetrisation above. That is
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:ForcedGeodesicEquation}
m \left( g_{jk} \ddot{x}^j  + \Gamma_{k,ij} \dot{x}^i \dot{x}^j \right)
 = \mathfrak{F}_k
\end{equation}
and thus we reobtain the geodesic equation.

More precisely, Eq.~\ref{Eq:ForcedGeodesicEquation} is the \emph{forced}
geodesic equation; it is the geodesic equation when $\mathfrak F_k=0$.
Contract with $g^{lk}$ and write $\Gamma^l_{ij}:=g^{lk}\Gamma_{k,ij}$,
$\mathfrak F^l:=g^{lk}\mathfrak F_k$:
\[
 m\bigl(\ddot x^l+\Gamma^l_{ij}\dot x^i\dot x^j\bigr)=\mathfrak F^l ,
\]
that is, $m\,\nabla_{\dot x}\dot x=\mathfrak F^\sharp$ with $\nabla$ the
Levi-Civita connection. The $\Gamma$ terms are not forces. They come from
differentiating the coordinate basis, so they appear in flat space in
curvilinear coordinates and vanish in Cartesian ones. The cylindrical
example below shows them as the centrifugal and Coriolis terms.

Two remarks on the hypotheses. If $m=m(t)$, an extra
$\dot m\,g_{kj}\dot x^j$ appears and Eq.~\ref{Eq:ForcedGeodesicEquation}
fails as written. A particle of changing mass is not a closed system;
Section~\ref{topic:vehicle-acceleration} treats the rocket correctly. And
absorbing a constant mass into the metric, $G_{ij}:=m\,g_{ij}$, changes
nothing: $\Gamma_{k,ij}[G]=m\,\Gamma_{k,ij}[g]$ and
$\Gamma^l_{ij}[G]=\Gamma^l_{ij}[g]$.

From here on no potential is carried explicitly, so $\mathfrak F=f$ is
written for the total force covector.

\subsection{Promotion and demotion: the musical isomorphisms}
\begin{definition}
For a Riemannian metric $g$, define
$\flat:T_xM\to T^*_xM$ by $v^\flat=g(v,\cdot)$, so
$(v^\flat)_i=g_{ij}v^j$, and $\sharp:T^*_xM\to T_xM$ by
$(\alpha^\sharp)^i=g^{ij}\alpha_j$, where $g^{ij}g_{jk}=\delta^i_k$. The two
are mutually inverse.
\end{definition}

So the force $1$-form can be promoted to a vector field $f^\sharp$, and
Newton's second law reads either $m(\nabla_{\dot x}\dot x)^\flat=f$ or
$m\nabla_{\dot x}\dot x=f^\sharp$. The promotion uses the metric, and the two
objects transform differently. In Cartesian coordinates on Euclidean
$\R^3$, $g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}$, so $f$ and $f^\sharp$ have equal components;
that is why engineering mechanics can treat force as a vector. In
cylindrical coordinates $(r,\theta,z)$,
$g=\operatorname{diag}(1,\;r^2,\;1)$, so $f_\theta=r^2f^\theta$, and the
covariant, contravariant and \emph{physical} components
$f_{(\theta)}=f_\theta/r=r f^\theta$ are three different numbers.

\subsection{Impulse is the increment of a covector}
The impulse $J=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}f(t)\,dt$ has a problem: $f(t)\in
T^*_{x(t)}M$, and covectors at different points lie in different vector
spaces. Making $J$ well defined needs one of
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
\item a flat space with a globally parallel (inertial Cartesian) frame,
      which identifies all fibres;
\item a connection, transporting the covectors to one point; or
\item contraction with a covariantly constant vector field first, which
      makes the integrand a scalar.
\end{itemize}
Rocket performance uses the first. The scalar total impulse is the third.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:impulse-momentum}
In Cartesian coordinates on Euclidean $\R^3$, $g$ is constant, hence
$\frac{\partial T}{\partial x^k}=0$ and the Euler--Lagrange equation reduces to
$\dot p_k=f_k$. Integrating,
$J=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}f\,dt=p(t_1)-p(t_0)$, an identity in $T^*\R^3$.
\end{proposition}

\noindent With $g_{ij}=\delta_{ij}$, every
$\frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial x^k}$ and every $\Gamma_{k,ij}$ vanishes, so
Eqs.~\ref{Eq:EulerLagrangeEquationForm2} and \ref{Eq:ForcedGeodesicEquation}
both become $\dot p_k=f_k$ with $p_k=m\,\delta_{kj}\dot x^j$. Integrating
slot by slot, $J_k=p_k(t_1)-p_k(t_0)$. The inertial frame identifies every
fibre $T^*_{x(t)}\R^3$ with one copy of $(\R^3)^*$, which is what makes
these three integrals the components of one covector.

\subsubsection*{The cylindrical case, explicitly}
Take $T=\tfrac12m(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2+\dot z^2)$. Then
Eq.~\ref{Eq:MomentumOfPointParticle} gives
\[
 p_r=m\dot r,\qquad p_\theta=mr^2\dot\theta,\qquad p_z=m\dot z,
\]
and, since $\frac{\partial g_{\theta\theta}}{\partial r}=2r$ is the only
nonvanishing metric derivative,
Eq.~\ref{Eq:PartialDerivativeOfPointParticleKineticEnergy} gives
$\frac{\partial T}{\partial r}=mr\dot\theta^2$ and
$\frac{\partial T}{\partial\theta}=\frac{\partial T}{\partial z}=0$.
Equation~\ref{Eq:EulerLagrangeEquationForm2} then reads
\[
\begin{aligned}
 r:&\quad m\ddot r-mr\dot\theta^2=f_r,\\
 \theta:&\quad \frac{d}{dt}\bigl(mr^2\dot\theta\bigr)
   =m\bigl(r^2\ddot\theta+2r\dot r\dot\theta\bigr)=f_\theta .
\end{aligned}
\]
The centrifugal term is the $\frac{\partial T}{\partial r}$ contribution, and
$2r\dot r\dot\theta$ is the Coriolis term. The forced geodesic form agrees:
$\Gamma_{r,\theta\theta}=-\tfrac12\frac{\partial g_{\theta\theta}}{\partial r}=-r$
and $\Gamma_{\theta,r\theta}=\Gamma_{\theta,\theta r}=r$, all others zero, so
Eq.~\ref{Eq:ForcedGeodesicEquation} gives
$m(\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2)=f_r$ and
$m(r^2\ddot\theta+2r\dot r\dot\theta)=f_\theta$.

The lower index identifies the quantity: $p_\theta=mr^2\dot\theta$ is the
angular momentum and $f_\theta$ is the torque, each with an extra factor of
$r$ relative to the physical components $c_\theta=r\dot\theta$ and
$f_{(\theta)}=f_\theta/r$. Hill and Peterson's
$rF_\theta=\delta m\,\tfrac{d}{dt}(rc_\theta)$ \cite[Eq.~7.5]{hillpeterson}
is $\dot p_\theta=f_\theta$ in physical components, since
$rc_\theta=r^2\dot\theta=p_\theta/m$ and their $rF_\theta$ is our $f_\theta$.
Because $\frac{\partial T}{\partial r}\neq0$, the componentwise
$\int f\,dt=\Delta p$ is false in these coordinates.

\subsection{From the covector to Sutton's total impulse}
Sutton defines total impulse by $I_t=\int_0^t F\,dt$
\cite[Eq.~2--1]{sutton}, with $F$ a scalar. If $\hat e$ is a fixed unit
direction (the thrust axis) and $F=f(\hat e)$, then
\[
 I_t=\int_0^{t}f(\hat e)\,dt=J(\hat e),
\]
one component of the impulse covector. Evaluating $J(\hat e)$ needs no
metric; normalising $\hat e=e/\sqrt{g(e,e)}$ does. Four assumptions separate
this from the general statement.
\begin{description}\itemsep2pt
\item[A1] Flat space, inertial Cartesian frame, so $J$ exists
  (Prop.~\ref{prop:impulse-momentum}).
\item[A2] The thrust direction $\hat e$ is constant over $[0,t]$.
\item[A3] $F\ge0$, the thrust magnitude along that axis.
\item[A4] Start and stop transients are negligible, where Eq.~2--2 is used.
\end{description}

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:scalar-bound}
$\lVert J\rVert\le\int_0^t\lVert f\rVert\,dt$, using the Euclidean dual norm.
Equality holds exactly when the nonzero force covectors lie on one common
ray almost everywhere in time. A change of thrust direction during nonzero
thrust, on a set of times with nonzero impulse contribution, makes the
scalar integral strictly exceed $\lVert J\rVert$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
If $J\ne0$, set $u=J^\sharp/\lVert J\rVert$, so $g(u,u)=1$, and use the
pointwise Cauchy--Schwarz inequality:
\[
 \lVert J\rVert=J(u)
 =\int_0^t f(u)\,dt
 \le\int_0^t\lVert f\rVert\lVert u\rVert\,dt
 =\int_0^t\lVert f\rVert\,dt.
\]
Equality requires the nonnegative integrand $\lVert f\rVert-f(u)$ to
vanish almost everywhere, that is, $f=\lambda(t)u^\flat$ with
$\lambda\ge0$. Conversely, such an $f$ gives
$J=(\int_0^t\lambda\,dt)\,u^\flat$, with equal norms. If $J=0$, equality
requires $\int_0^t\lVert f\rVert\,dt=0$, so $f=0$ almost everywhere.
\end{proof}

\noindent The scalar total impulse bounds the magnitude of the thrust
impulse. Converting either into a vehicle $\Delta u$ needs the mass history:
Section~\ref{topic:vehicle-acceleration} applies the same inequality to
$f/M$.

Sutton's Eq.~2--2, $I_t=Ft$, holds $F$ constant; A4 lets the transients,
during which $F$ is not constant, be left out rather than modelled. For
specific impulse Sutton writes
\[
 I_s=\frac{\int_0^t F\,dt}{g_0\int_0^t\dot m\,dt}
 \qquad\text{\cite[Eq.~2--3]{sutton}},
\]
a ratio of two integrals.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:weighted-mean}
Let $\dot m(t)>0$ be the propellant consumption rate over the averaging
interval. With the instantaneous $I_s(t)=F/(\dot m g_0)$,
\[
 \frac{\int F\,dt}{g_0\int\dot m\,dt}
 =\frac{\int I_s(t)\,\dot m\,dt}{\int \dot m\,dt},
\]
the \emph{mass-flow-weighted} mean of $I_s$. It equals the time average
exactly when the time covariance of $I_s$ and $\dot m$ vanishes. Constant
$\dot m$ is sufficient but not necessary.
\end{proposition}

\noindent For an interval of length $\tau>0$, let
$\langle h\rangle_t:=\frac1\tau\int_0^\tau h\,dt$ and
$\operatorname{Cov}_t(I_s,\dot m)
:=\langle I_s\dot m\rangle_t-\langle I_s\rangle_t\langle\dot m\rangle_t$. Then
\begin{equation}\label{Eq:SpecificImpulseMeanDifference}
 \frac{\int_0^\tau I_s\dot m\,dt}{\int_0^\tau\dot m\,dt}
      -\langle I_s\rangle_t
  =\frac{\langle I_s\dot m\rangle_t
          -\langle I_s\rangle_t\langle\dot m\rangle_t}
         {\langle\dot m\rangle_t}
   =\frac{\operatorname{Cov}_t(I_s,\dot m)}{\langle\dot m\rangle_t}.
\end{equation}
A constant $I_s=I_0$ with any positive variable $\dot m$ gives both means
equal to $I_0$, so constant mass flow is not necessary. Sutton's Eq.~2--5,
$I_s=F/(\dot m g_0)$, is the constant-$F$, constant-$\dot m$ case, and
Eq.~2--4, $I_s=I_t/(m_pg_0)$, follows from $\int\dot m\,dt=m_p$.

Two further points. First, $g_0=9.80665\,\mathrm{m/s^2}$ is a
\emph{defined} constant, not the local gravitational acceleration; in orbit
$m_pg_0$ ``does not represent the weight'' \cite[Section 2.1]{sutton}. So
$I_s$ in seconds is a velocity divided by a conventional constant, not a
duration. Second, the effective exhaust velocity
\[
 u_e=I_sg_0=F/\dot m \qquad\text{(Sutton's $c$, \cite[Eq.~2--6]{sutton})}
\]
divides a force by a mass flow rate and calls the result a velocity. Force
is a covector and velocity a vector, so the equality is really
$u_e\,\hat e=(f/\dot m)^\sharp$, the musical isomorphism applied silently. For
thrust along a unit $\hat e$, $f=F\hat e^\flat$, and
$(f/\dot m)^\sharp=\frac{F}{\dot m}(\hat e^\flat)^\sharp=u_e\,\hat e$.
The pairing is metric-free. The magnitudes and unit directions used in both
$I_t$ and $u_e$ carry the metric.

\subsection{Where the thrust covector comes from}
The $f$ in the vehicle equation of motion is the resultant of surface
tractions, pressure and viscous, over the wetted boundary. Traction is
naturally a covector-valued $2$-form; Frankel pairs it with a deformation
$1$-form to give a scalar $3$-form of work
\cite[Section on Elasticity and Stresses]{frankel}. Sutton's
momentum-flux expression for thrust follows from a control-volume balance,
derived in Section~\ref{topic:sutton-definitions}
(Eq.~\ref{Eq:ThrustEquation}). Because the vehicle loses mass,
$F=d(Mu)/dt$ is \emph{not} its equation of motion.
Section~\ref{topic:vehicle-acceleration} derives the correct one,
$M\,du/dt=F-D-Mg\sin\theta$.

\subsection{Notation: exhaust velocities}
Our effective exhaust velocity is $u_e:=F/\dot m$, and the nozzle exit
velocity relative to the vehicle is $u_{\rm exh}$, with exit pressure
$p_{\rm exh}$, ambient pressure $p_a$ and exit area $A_e$. The books
collide with this and with each other.
\begin{description}\itemsep2pt
\item[Sutton] $c$ is the effective exhaust velocity \cite[Eq.~2--6]{sutton}
  and $v_2$ the exit velocity; $c^*$ is characteristic velocity.
\item[Hill and Peterson] $u_e$ is the \emph{actual} exit velocity, our
  $u_{\rm exh}$, and $u_{\rm eq}$ the effective one, our $u_e$
  \cite[Eq.~(10.5)]{hillpeterson}. Their $c$ (and $\mathbf c$) is the
  absolute fluid velocity, with components $c_r,c_\theta,c_z$ in the
  turbomachinery chapters, which we keep; speed of sound is $a$.
\end{description}
Read Hill and Peterson's $u_e$ as our $u_{\rm exh}$.

\subsection{An erratum}
Sutton's worked Example~2--1 (printed p.~31 / PDF p.~55) prints
\[
 F=I_sg_0=23.3\times2354.4=54{,}936\ \mathrm{N},
\]
checked against the source PDF's own text layer. The line has two defects.
First, $I_sg_0=2354.4\ \mathrm{m/s}$ is the effective exhaust velocity $u_e$
(the book computes it as its $c$ one sentence earlier), so the equation sets
a force equal to a velocity; Eq.~2--6 gives $F=\dot mu_e=\dot mI_sg_0$. Second,
$23.3\times2354.4=54{,}857.5$; the printed $54{,}936$ comes from the
unrounded $\dot m=70/3$, since $2354.4\times70/3=54{,}936$ exactly. Every
downstream quantity is correct, including $I_t=164{,}808\ \mathrm{N\,s}$
(confirmed by $I_sm_pg_0=240\times70\times9.81$) and the final acceleration
$422.58\ \mathrm{m/s^2}=43.08\,g_0$. Dimensional analysis already catches
the missing factor:
\[
 [I_sg_0]=\mathrm{s}\,\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s^2}}
        =\frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}},\qquad
 [\dot m I_sg_0]=\frac{\mathrm{kg}}{\mathrm{s}}
                  \frac{\mathrm{m}}{\mathrm{s}}=\mathrm{N}.
\]
The distinction $f\in T^*M$ versus $u_e\,\hat e=(f/\dot m)^\sharp\in TM$ is a
separate geometric check. The metric converts tensor type after division
by $\dot m$; it does not supply the missing units.
