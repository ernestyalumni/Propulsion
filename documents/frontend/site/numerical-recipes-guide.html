§0How to read this book

Read Numerical Recipes as a map of the territory, not as a source of algorithms to copy.

That framing matters, because it is the consensus critique of the book and it is largely fair:

Reviews by scientists and engineers tend to extol the broad scope and convenience, while reviews by numerical analysts are very critical of the quality of the discussions and the codes. — “Why not use Numerical Recipes?”

Two specific charges land directly on our use cases. First, Chapter 17 on ODEs “describes numerical methods from the viewpoint of 1970.” That is the chapter closest to spacecraft propagation, so it is the one that most needs a modern companion. Second, the special-function approximations “shouldn’t be trusted beyond 6 digits” — fine for a demo, not fine for an ephemeris or a covariance.

What NR is uniquely good at, and why it is still worth the read: it tells you why one algorithm beats another on real hardware — conditioning, cancellation, step-size policy, when to give up and restart. That reasoning is the durable content. The FORTRAN-in-C++ implementations are the disposable part.

The structural problem in the shipped code

Open NR_C301/code/stepperdopr5.h — 142 lines. Two mathematical objects are buried inside function bodies as unnamed literals.

The Butcher tableau. a21 … a76, c2 … c5, e1 … e7 are declared static const Doub inside StepperDopr5<D>::dy(). The tableau is the identity of the method — its order, its stability region, its error estimator. As written you cannot name it, unit-test its order conditions, print it, or swap it for DOPR853 without editing a function body.

The step-size control law. beta=0.0, alpha=0.2-beta*0.75, safe=0.9, minscale=0.2, maxscale=10.0 are static const inside Controller::success(). Note beta=0.0: that single literal silently disables Lund stabilization, turning a PI controller into a plain I controller. A design decision with real consequences is expressed as a magic number three scopes deep.

That is what “the principles are buried in the code” means concretely. The book’s prose explains PI step control; the code hides whether it is even switched on.

The fix is already proven in Cosmos

Propulsion/Cosmos/Source/Numerical/ODE/ is exactly the right decomposition, and it already exists:

The pattern is: every noun in the mathematics becomes a named type; every magic constant becomes a named, asserted, injected parameter; every verb becomes a testable free function or a small class. Chapter 17 is done. The reading order below is essentially the queue for applying that same pattern to the rest of the book.

Substitute for Arc A

Chapter 17 is the weakest chapter relative to the state of the art. Read it for intuition, then read Hairer & Wanner for the actual theory. The single most important idea NR omits: for a Hamiltonian system like an orbit, a symplectic integrator has bounded energy error over exponentially long times, while DOPRI5 — no matter how tight the tolerance — drifts secularly.

The first-principles protocol, per section

Reading a section is not finished until all seven exist. This is what keeps the rewrite from aping the book.

  1. Physics first. The system and its governing equations in the library's own units, frames, and groups.

  2. The structure exploited. Positive definite ⇒ Cholesky without pivoting; Hamiltonian ⇒ symplectic; stiff ⇒ implicit; no y′ ⇒ Störmer.

  3. Named objects. Every noun a type, every constant an asserted parameter, every verb a testable function.

  4. Derive, then compare. Write the recurrence from the equations, then read NR's prose. Never open NR_C301/code while writing.

  5. Tests from the mathematics. Order conditions, reconstruction to machine precision, bounded drift, observed convergence order, cross-language golden vectors.

  6. Citation sidecar with section, page, equation tags, and the substitute reference where NR is dated.

  7. Ledger entry in READING-LEDGER.md: what NR got right, what aged, what resulted.

Source of truth for this section: documents/research/numerical-recipes-rewrite/ROADMAP.md.

§3What to read this week

Roughly 60 printed pages, in dependency order. It takes you from “I have an integrator” to “I know what I need for a navigation filter.”

  1. §1.1 8pdf 32 — four pages. Non-negotiable.

  2. §17.2 910pdf 934 — read it with ComputePIStepSize.h open beside it. You will see your own design decisions justified in the prose, and you will see the beta=0.0 choice the NR code makes silently.

  3. §17.4 928pdf 952 — Störmer, against your StormerMethods/.

  4. §3.0–3.3 110–124pdf 134–148 — the interpolation theory behind dense output.

  5. §2.9 + §2.10 100–106pdf 124–130 — Cholesky and QR. The gateway to the estimation work, and only about six pages.

  6. §1.3–1.5 17–36pdf 41–60 — last, as an artifact of 2007 C++ thinking. Read adversarially.

§4Rewrite queue

In dependency order, matching the arcs. Chapter 17 is done in C++. The Rust workspace at Cosmos/Rust started 2026-09-01 with the PI step-size controller as a golden-vector twin of the C++ and a Cholesky factorization type from first principles; this is what follows.

  1. Numerical/LinearAlgebra/ — Cholesky, QRDecomposition, SVD, LUDecomposition. Named factorization types that own their factors, not void functions with out-parameters. Follow NVector’s lead in Algebra/Modules/Vectors/.

  2. Numerical/Interpolation/ — extend what exists: CubicSpline, PolynomialInterpolation, RationalInterpolation. Lookup.h already generalizes NR’s Base_interp::locate/hunt.

  3. Numerical/RootFinding/ — Bisection, Brent, NewtonRaphson, LineSearch, GloballyConvergentNewton. NR fuses the line-search policy into lnsrch; separate it, the same way ComputePIStepSize was separated from the stepper.

  4. Numerical/Quadrature/ — the Quadrature base in §4.2 is one of the few genuinely good abstractions in the book. Keep the idea, fix the naming.

  5. Estimation/ — not from NR. Build on the Cholesky / QR types from step 1.

  6. Numerical/Optimization/ — already started. mins.h is still NR-shaped; that file is the obvious next cleanup.

Licensing

The NR code is not freely redistributable — one of the standing criticisms of the book. Since we are deriving from published mathematics rather than copying their source, the rewrite should be written from the prose and the equations, not by transliterating NR_C301/code/*.h. Given how much of the mathematics is buried in their literals, that is also the only way to get a design worth having.

§5Beyond Numerical Recipes

Where the book has aged, these are the replacements — ordered by how much they matter for this work.

Tier 1 · Buy these

  • Hairer, Nørsett & Wanner, Solving Ordinary Differential Equations I & II

    The definitive treatment. Dense output, embedded pairs, order conditions, and PI step-size control are all developed properly here — this is where NR §17.2’s ideas actually come from. Volume II covers stiff and differential-algebraic problems.

  • Hairer, Lubich & Wanner, Geometric Numerical Integration

    Symplectic and symmetric methods, problems on manifolds. The single highest-value book on this list for spacecraft simulation — the theory of why your orbit shouldn’t lose energy. Hairer’s lecture notes are free.

  • Montenbruck & Gill, Satellite Orbits: Models, Methods and Applications

    The best marriage of numerical methods to actual orbit propagation — force models, numerical integration, and estimation in one volume, at implementation level of detail.

  • Markley & Crassidis, Fundamentals of Spacecraft Attitude Determination and Control

    Springer, 2014. Fills NR’s total gap on estimation: MEKF, quaternion error states, attitude filters.

  • Higham, Accuracy and Stability of Numerical Algorithms

    SIAM, 2nd ed. Chapter 2 on floating point is described as possibly “the single most useful chapter in the book.” This is Tier-0 material done rigorously.

Tier 2 · Read for specific arcs

  • Trefethen & Bau, Numerical Linear Algebra

    For Arc B. Short, and the best book on conditioning and stability of factorizations.

  • Tapley, Schutz & Born, Statistical Orbit Determination

    2004. Batch and sequential OD in full detail — the operational reference.

  • Vallado, Fundamentals of Astrodynamics and Applications

    Practitioner’s handbook with companion code. Complements Montenbruck’s rigor with breadth.

  • Battin, An Introduction to the Mathematics and Methods of Astrodynamics

    Deepest on the mathematics — Lambert, universal variables. Hard going, but the classic.

  • Betts, “Survey of Numerical Methods for Trajectory Optimization”

    JGCD 21(2):193–207, 1998, plus his book Practical Methods for Optimal Control Using Nonlinear Programming. The bridge from NR ch. 18 to modern direct collocation.

  • Law, Combustion Physics

    For Arc D, paired with the SUNDIALS / CVODE documentation — BDF methods are the workhorse for stiff chemistry.

  • Crassidis, Markley & Cheng, “Survey of Nonlinear Attitude Estimation Methods”

    JGCD, 2007. The map of the estimator landscape.

  • Markley, “Multiplicative vs. Additive Filtering for Spacecraft Attitude Determination”

    NASA GSFC. Short, free, and settles the question of how to filter a quaternion.

Tier 3 · C++ architecture for the rewrite

  • P1673 — “A free function linear algebra interface based on the BLAS,” and P1674

    Read these as design documents. Their core argument is exactly our thesis: a free-function interface “clearly separates algorithms from data structures.” Built on mdspan (C++23). This is the standards-committee answer to the question NR answered badly in §1.4.

  • std::mdspan

    The modern replacement for NR’s MatDoub. Non-owning, layout- and accessor-parameterized; solves the “don’t copy large objects” problem NR worked around with out-parameters.

  • Eigen, and expression templates

    Study Eigen’s architecture even if we don’t adopt it — the reference implementation of lazy evaluation for linear algebra.

  • Falcou et al., “The Numerical Template Toolbox: A Modern C++ Design for Scientific Computing”

    A full worked example of the design problem we are solving.

  • Goldberg, “What Every Computer Scientist Should Know About Floating-Point Arithmetic”

    ACM Computing Surveys, 1991. Free, and the standard reference behind Tier 0.
